# Attack 04 — Derived categories, Hodge isometries and real multiplication on K3 surfaces

**Verdict in one paragraph.** The top target (every element of $E=\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})$, $E$ totally real of degree $>1$, is algebraic on $S\times S$) is **not** proved here. What *is* proved completely: (A) a sharp obstruction theorem (§3): the entire toolbox of this strategy — rational Hodge isometries (Buskin), twisted Fourier–Mukai partners (Huybrechts–Stellari, Huybrechts), moduli-space correspondences (Mukai), Hodge isometries of $K3^{[n]}$-type (Markman), and finite/dominant rational maps — only ever produces Hodge *similitudes* of transcendental lattices, and the $\mathbb Q$-span of all composites of similitudes from $T(S)$ back to $T(S)$ lies in the compositum $E^{(2)}$ of the quadratic subfields of $E$. Hence this strategy can never prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ unless $E$ is multiquadratic, and can prove nothing at all when $[E:\mathbb Q]$ is odd (such K3 surfaces exist: Bayer-Fluckiger–van Geemen–Schütt 2024). (B) For real quadratic $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ an exact reduction (§4): the RM classes are algebraic iff one Hodge similitude of multiplier $D$ out of $T(S)$ is algebraic, and a sufficient condition — a dominant rational map of degree $Dc^{2}$ between $S$ (or any K3 surface isogenous to $S$) and a K3 surface — which contains Varesco's 2023 theorem (symplectic automorphisms of order $2,3$) as the Galois case. (C) For every totally positive $q\in E$ and $\rho(S)\ge 3$ there is a projective K3 surface $S_q$ with $T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}\cong (T(S)_{\mathbb Q},(x,qy))$ (§5); the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ is equivalent to the algebraicity of enough Hodge morphisms $T(S)\to T(S_q)$, and these are *never* similitudes unless $q\in\mathbb Q\cdot E^{*2}$ — which is exactly why no known technique reaches them. Gaps: §6. Checklist: §7.

## 1. Setting, notation, standing facts

Throughout, $S$ is a complex projective K3 surface, $H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\cong\Lambda_{K3}=U^{3}\oplus E_8(-1)^{2}$ with the intersection form $(\,,)$, $NS(S)=H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\cap H^{1,1}(S)$, $\rho=\operatorname{rk}NS(S)\ge 1$, $T(S)=NS(S)^{\perp}$ the transcendental lattice, $r=\operatorname{rk}T(S)=22-\rho$. Write $T=T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$: a rational Hodge structure of weight 2 with $h^{2,0}=1$, polarized by $(\,,)$, which has signature $(2,r-2)$ on $T_{\mathbb R}$, is positive definite on the real 2-plane $P=T_{\mathbb R}\cap(H^{2,0}\oplus H^{0,2})$ and negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}=P^{\perp}$. "Algebraic" always means: in the image of the cycle class map with $\mathbb Q$-coefficients. For a correspondence $\Gamma\in CH^{*}(X\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ we write $\Gamma_*$ for its action on cohomology and ${}^{t}\Gamma$ for its transpose; composites of algebraic correspondences are algebraic and act by the composite map (Fulton, *Intersection theory*, Ch. 16).

**Fact 1.1 (Zarhin 1983).** $T$ is an irreducible Hodge structure; $E:=\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)$ is a number field; the adjoint involution $e\mapsto e^{\dagger}$, defined by $(ex,y)=(x,e^{\dagger}y)$, is either the identity — then $E$ is totally real (*real multiplication*, RM) — or non-trivial — then $E$ is a CM field and $\dagger$ is complex conjugation (*complex multiplication*, CM). (Zarhin, *Hodge groups of K3 surfaces*, Crelle 341 (1983), Thm. 1.5.1 and §1.6; Huybrechts, *Lectures on K3 surfaces*, Ch. 3.3.)

**Fact 1.2 (RM: decomposition at the real places, and $m\ge 3$).** Assume RM; put $d=[E:\mathbb Q]$, $m=\dim_E T$, so $r=md$. Over $\mathbb R$, $T_{\mathbb R}=\bigoplus_{\sigma:E\hookrightarrow\mathbb R}T_\sigma$, $T_\sigma=T\otimes_{E,\sigma}\mathbb R$, and the summands are mutually orthogonal: for $x\in T_\sigma$, $y\in T_\tau$ and $e\in E$, $\sigma(e)(x,y)=(ex,y)=(x,ey)=\tau(e)(x,y)$, so $(x,y)=0$ if $\sigma\ne\tau$. The line $H^{2,0}$ is an $E$-eigenline on which $E$ acts through one embedding $\sigma_1$ (the eigenvalue is real because $E$ is totally real), and $H^{0,2}=\overline{H^{2,0}}$ has the same eigenvalue; hence $P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$. Consequently $(\,,)|_{T_{\sigma_1}}$ has signature $(2,m-2)$, and $(\,,)|_{T_\sigma}$ is negative definite for $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$. Each $T_\sigma$ is of type $(1,1)$ for $\sigma\neq\sigma_1$. Finally $m\ge 3$ (van Geemen, Michigan Math. J. 56 (2008), Lemma 3.2). *Proof for completeness.* $m\ge 2$ since $P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$. Suppose $m=2$. Let $\psi:T\times T\to E$ be the $E$-bilinear form with $\operatorname{tr}_{E/\mathbb Q}\circ\psi=(\,,)$ (it exists and is symmetric because every $e\in E$ is self-adjoint). Its discriminant $\delta\in E^{*}/E^{*2}$ is totally positive ($\psi_{\sigma_1}$ is positive definite on $T_{\sigma_1}=P$, $\psi_\sigma$ negative definite for $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$), so $L:=E(\sqrt{-\delta})$ is a CM field and $(T,\psi)\cong(L,\,a\cdot N_{L/E})$ for some $a\in E^{*}$. The Hodge structure is a homomorphism $h:\mathbb C^{*}\to SO(T_{\mathbb R})$ which acts on $T_{\sigma_1}=P$ through $SO(P)=SO(\psi_{\sigma_1})$ and trivially on the other $T_\sigma$; each $SO(\psi_\sigma)$ is the group of norm-one elements of $L_\sigma=L\otimes_{E,\sigma}\mathbb R\cong\mathbb C$ acting by multiplication, which commutes with multiplication by $L$. So $L\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)=E$, contradicting $[L:E]=2$. $\square$

Thus RM forces $r=md\ge 6$, $\rho\le 16$, $d\le 7$; in particular no K3 surface with $\rho\ge 17$ (the Shioda–Inose / Morrison range where the Kuga–Satake correspondence is known to be algebraic) has RM.

**Lemma 1.3 (Hodge classes on $S\times S$).** $\operatorname{Hdg}^2(S\times S)=\mathbb Q[S\times\mathrm{pt}]\oplus\mathbb Q[\mathrm{pt}\times S]\oplus(NS(S)_{\mathbb Q}\otimes NS(S)_{\mathbb Q})\oplus\{\gamma_e: e\in E\}$, where $\gamma_e\in T\otimes T\subset H^4(S\times S,\mathbb Q)$ is the unique class with $(\gamma_e)_*|_{T}=e$ and $(\gamma_e)_*|_{NS}=0$. The first three summands are algebraic. Hence, with
$$E^{\mathrm{alg}}:=\{e\in E:\ \gamma_e \text{ is algebraic}\}=\{e\in E:\ e=\Gamma_*|_{T}\text{ for some algebraic }\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times S)_{\mathbb Q}\},$$
the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ (in all codimensions) iff $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$; and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a subfield of $E$.

*Proof.* Since $H^{\mathrm{odd}}(S)=0$, $H^4(S\times S)=H^0\otimes H^4\oplus H^2\otimes H^2\oplus H^4\otimes H^0$; the outer summands are spanned by $[\mathrm{pt}\times S]$, $[S\times\mathrm{pt}]$. The map $\gamma\mapsto\gamma_*=p_{2*}(\gamma\cup p_1^{*}(\cdot))$ is an isomorphism of Hodge structures $H^2\otimes H^2\cong\operatorname{End}(H^2(S,\mathbb Q))(-2)$, so Hodge classes in $H^2\otimes H^2$ correspond to $\operatorname{End}_{HS}(H^2(S,\mathbb Q))=\operatorname{End}(NS_{\mathbb Q})\oplus E$ (there are no non-zero Hodge morphisms between $NS_{\mathbb Q}$, a sum of copies of $\mathbb Q(-1)$, and the irreducible $T$ with $h^{2,0}\ne0$). Under this correspondence $NS\otimes NS\mapsto\operatorname{End}(NS)$ and $T\otimes T\mapsto\operatorname{End}(T)$. Products of divisors $p_1^{*}D\cdot p_2^{*}D'$ span $NS\otimes NS$. The projector $\pi_{NS}=\sum_{i,j}g^{ij}\,p_1^{*}D_i\cdot p_2^{*}D_j$ ($D_i$ a basis of $NS_{\mathbb Q}$, $(g^{ij})$ the inverse Gram matrix) is algebraic and $(\pi_{NS})_*$ is the orthogonal projection onto $NS_{\mathbb Q}$; hence $\pi_T:=\Delta-\pi_{NS}-[\mathrm{pt}\times S]-[S\times\mathrm{pt}]$ is algebraic with $(\pi_T)_*=$ projection onto $T$. If $\Gamma$ is algebraic with $\Gamma_*|_T=e$ then $\gamma_e=\pi_T\circ\Gamma\circ\pi_T$ is algebraic; the converse is trivial. Codimension-1 and codimension-3 Hodge classes on the fourfold $S\times S$ are algebraic by Lefschetz (1,1) and hard Lefschetz. Finally $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ contains $1$ (the diagonal), is a $\mathbb Q$-subspace, and is closed under composition (composition of correspondences), so it is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb Q$-subalgebra of the field $E$, i.e. a subfield. $\square$

**Fact 1.4 (Buskin; Huybrechts).** For projective K3 surfaces $S_1,S_2$, every Hodge isometry $\phi:H^2(S_1,\mathbb Q)\to H^2(S_2,\mathbb Q)$ is induced by an algebraic class $Z_\phi\in H^{2,2}(S_1\times S_2,\mathbb Q)$ (Buskin, Crelle 755 (2019), Thm. 1.1; second proof Huybrechts, Comment. Math. Helv. 94 (2019), Cor. 0.4). Consequently every Hodge isometry $\psi:T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ is algebraic: by Witt cancellation ($H^2(S_i,\mathbb Q)\cong\Lambda_{K3}\otimes\mathbb Q$ for both $i$) there is an isometry $NS(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\cong NS(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$, so $\psi$ extends to a Hodge isometry $\tilde\psi$ of $H^2$'s, and $\pi_{T_2}\circ Z_{\tilde\psi}\circ\pi_{T_1}$ is algebraic and induces $\psi$ (this corollary is stated in Buskin's paper). For $\rho\ge 11$ this is Mukai (1987) and Nikulin (1987).

**Lemma 1.5 (adjoints are algebraic).** (a) Let $S,Y$ be projective K3 surfaces and $\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$. Then $({}^t\Gamma)_*:H^2(Y)\to H^2(S)$ is the adjoint of $\Gamma_*$ for the intersection pairings: $(\Gamma_*x,y)_Y=(x,({}^t\Gamma)_*y)_S$. (b) Let $Y$ be a projective hyper-Kähler manifold of dimension $2n$ with Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form $q_Y$, Fujiki constant $c_Y$ ($\int_Y\alpha^{2n}=c_Y\,q_Y(\alpha)^n$), ample class $h$, and $\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$. Then $\Gamma_*(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})\subseteq T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}:=NS(Y)_{\mathbb Q}^{\perp}$ and the adjoint of $\Gamma_*|_{T(S)}$ with respect to $(\,,)_S$ and $q_Y$ is induced by the algebraic correspondence $\Gamma^{\dagger}:=\frac{2n-1}{c_Y\,q_Y(h)^{n-1}}\;{}^t\Gamma\circ\Delta_*(h^{2n-2})$.

*Proof.* (a) is the projection formula. (b) $\Gamma_*$ is a morphism of Hodge structures and $T(S)$ is irreducible and not of type $(1,1)$, so its image lies in the transcendental part $T(Y)$ (an irreducible sub-Hodge structure of $H^2(Y,\mathbb Q)=NS_{\mathbb Q}\oplus T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ with $h^{2,0}\ne 0$ maps isomorphically to $T(Y)$ because $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(Y),NS_{\mathbb Q})=0$). Polarizing the Fujiki relation gives, for $x,y\in h^{\perp}$, $\int_Y x\cdot y\cdot h^{2n-2}=\frac{c_Y}{2n-1}q_Y(h)^{n-1}q_Y(x,y)$ (in the sum over the $(2n)!$ pairings only those pairing $x$ with $y$ survive, $2n\,(2n-2)!$ of them). Hence for $s\in T(S)$, $y\in T(Y)$: $q_Y(\Gamma_*s,y)=\frac{2n-1}{c_Yq_Y(h)^{n-1}}\int_Y\Gamma_*s\cdot(y\cdot h^{2n-2})=\frac{2n-1}{c_Yq_Y(h)^{n-1}}\big(s,({}^t\Gamma)_*(y\cdot h^{2n-2})\big)_S$. $\square$

## 2. Norms of Hodge morphisms and the field $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$

**Definition 2.1.** Let $Y$ be a projective K3 surface or a projective hyper-Kähler manifold, $T_Y:=T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}=NS(Y)_{\mathbb Q}^{\perp}$ with its form $q_Y$ (intersection form, resp. Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form); $T_Y$ is an irreducible Hodge structure with $h^{2,0}=1$, polarized by $q_Y$ (same proof as for K3 surfaces: a sub-Hodge structure not containing $H^{2,0}$ is of type $(1,1)$, hence contained in $T_Y\cap T_Y^{\perp}=0$). For a morphism of Hodge structures $\phi:T\to T_Y$ let $\phi^{\dagger}:T_Y\to T$ be its adjoint, $q_Y(\phi x,y)=(x,\phi^{\dagger}y)$; it is again a morphism of Hodge structures (both forms are morphisms of Hodge structures to $\mathbb Q(-2)$ and are non-degenerate). The **norm** of $\phi$ is $N(\phi):=\phi^{\dagger}\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)=E$. We call $\phi$ a **Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$** if $q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=\lambda(x,y)$ for all $x,y$. We say $\phi$ is **algebraic** if $\phi=\Gamma_*|_T$ for an algebraic correspondence $\Gamma$.

**Lemma 2.2.** Assume RM. Let $\phi:T\to T_Y$ be a non-zero morphism of Hodge structures.
1. $\phi$ is an isomorphism, $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_Y)=\phi\cdot E$, and $N(\phi a)=a^{2}N(\phi)$ for $a\in E$.
2. $\phi$ is a Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda$ iff $N(\phi)=\lambda\in\mathbb Q$.
3. $N(\phi)$ is totally positive.
4. If $\phi$ is algebraic, so is $\phi^{\dagger}$, and $N(\phi)\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$.
5. $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is the subfield of $E$ generated by the norms $N(\phi)$ of all algebraic $\phi:T\to T_Y$ (all $Y$; it suffices to take $Y=S$).

*Proof.* (1) $T$ and $T_Y$ are irreducible, so a non-zero morphism is an isomorphism; then $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_Y)=\phi\circ\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)$; and $N(\phi a)=a^{\dagger}\phi^{\dagger}\phi a=a\,N(\phi)\,a$ since $a^{\dagger}=a$ and $E$ is commutative. (2) $q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=(x,N(\phi)y)$, and $(x,ey)=\lambda(x,y)$ for all $x,y$ iff $e=\lambda$. (3) The form $(x,y)':=q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=(x,N(\phi)y)$ is positive on $P$ (because $\phi$ maps $H^{2,0}$ to $H^{2,0}$, hence $P$ onto $P_Y$, where $q_Y$ is positive) and negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$ (mapped onto $T_Y{}^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$). On $T_\sigma$ one has $(x,N(\phi)y)=\sigma(N(\phi))(x,y)$ (Fact 1.2). For $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$, $T_\sigma\subset T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$ and both forms are negative definite there, so $\sigma(N(\phi))>0$. For $\sigma_1$ take $0\ne x\in P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$: $(x,x)>0$ and $(x,x)'>0$, so $\sigma_1(N(\phi))>0$. (4) Lemma 1.5: if $\phi=\Gamma_*|_T$ then $\phi^{\dagger}=(\Gamma^{\dagger})_*|_{T_Y}$ and $N(\phi)=(\Gamma^{\dagger}\circ\Gamma)_*|_T$. (5) Let $F$ be the subfield generated by the norms; $F\subseteq E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ by (4) and Lemma 1.3. Conversely, for $e\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ the maps $e,\,e+1:T\to T$ are algebraic with norms $e^{2},(e+1)^{2}$, so $2e=(e+1)^{2}-e^{2}-1\in F$. $\square$

**Remark 2.3 (the CM case, for contrast — known).** If $E$ is CM with maximal real subfield $E_0$, then $N(e)=\bar e e$ for $e\in E$, and the elements $u$ with $u\bar u=1$ are Hodge isometries of $T$, hence algebraic by Fact 1.4 (with $S_1=S_2=S$). They span $E$ over $\mathbb Q$: write $E=E_0(\sqrt{-\delta})$ with $\delta\in E_0$ totally positive; the $\mathbb Q$-span $F$ of the norm-one elements is closed under products and contains $1$, hence is a subfield; for $a\in E_0$ and $x=a+\sqrt{-\delta}$ the elements $u=x/\bar x$, $\bar u$ have norm one and $u+\bar u=2-4\delta/(a^{2}+\delta)$, so $(a^{2}+\delta)^{-1}\in F$, hence $a^{2}+\delta\in F$ for all $a\in E_0$, hence $2a+1=((a+1)^{2}+\delta)-(a^{2}+\delta)\in F$, so $E_0\subseteq F$; and $u-\bar u=4a\sqrt{-\delta}/(a^{2}+\delta)\in F$ with $a=1$ gives $\sqrt{-\delta}\in F$. So $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$: **the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ whenever $S$ has CM** (Ramón-Marí; Schlickewei, J. Algebra 324 (2010) for $\rho\ge 5$ via Mukai; unconditionally via Buskin 2019, who states this application). In the RM case the only Hodge isometries in $E$ are $\pm1$ ($e^{\dagger}e=e^{2}=1$), and *this is the whole difference between the two cases.*

## 3. Theorem A: the similitude obstruction

**Definition 3.1.** A *similitude chain* from $S$ to $S$ is a sequence $X_0=S,X_1,\dots,X_k=S$ of projective K3 surfaces and/or projective hyper-Kähler manifolds together with Hodge similitudes $\psi_i:T_{X_{i-1}}\to T_{X_i}$ ($i=1,\dots,k$; multipliers $\lambda_i\in\mathbb Q$, with respect to the intersection/BBF forms). Its *value* is $\psi_k\circ\dots\circ\psi_1\in E$. Let $\mathcal S(S)\subseteq E$ be the $\mathbb Q$-linear span of the values of all similitude chains. Let $E^{(2)}\subseteq E$ be the subfield generated by $\{e\in E:e^{2}\in\mathbb Q\}$; it is the compositum of the quadratic subfields of $E$, a multiquadratic field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{a_1},\dots,\sqrt{a_s})$ (Galois over $\mathbb Q$ with elementary abelian $2$-group).

**Theorem A.** Let $S$ be a projective K3 surface with real multiplication by $E$. Then $\mathcal S(S)$ is a subfield of $E$ and
$$\mathcal S(S)\subseteq E^{(2)}.$$
In particular: (a) if $[E:\mathbb Q]$ is odd, $\mathcal S(S)=\mathbb Q$; (b) if $E$ is not multiquadratic (e.g. any cubic, quintic or septic field, any quartic field that is not biquadratic, a cyclic sextic field, …), then $\mathcal S(S)\subsetneq E$.

*Proof.* Let $v=\psi_k\circ\dots\circ\psi_1$ be the value of a chain. Since $\psi_i^{\dagger}\psi_i=\lambda_i\cdot\mathrm{id}$, induction gives $v^{\dagger}v=\psi_1^{\dagger}\cdots\psi_k^{\dagger}\psi_k\cdots\psi_1=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_k\in\mathbb Q$. But $v\in E$ and $v^{\dagger}=v$ (RM), so $v^{2}\in\mathbb Q$ and $v\in E^{(2)}$. Hence $\mathcal S(S)\subseteq E^{(2)}$. The value of the concatenation of two chains is the product of the values, so $\mathcal S(S)$ is closed under multiplication; it contains $1$ (the chain of length $0$) and is a $\mathbb Q$-subspace; a finite-dimensional $\mathbb Q$-subalgebra of a field is a subfield. (a) A field of odd degree has no quadratic subfield, so $E^{(2)}=\mathbb Q$. (b) $E^{(2)}$ is generated by square roots of rational numbers, hence multiquadratic; $E^{(2)}=E$ forces $E$ to be multiquadratic. $\square$

**Lemma 3.2 (inventory: everything this strategy produces is a similitude).** Each of the following maps is a Hodge similitude between transcendental lattices (with respect to intersection/BBF forms), with the indicated multiplier:
1. Rational Hodge isometries $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ between projective K3 surfaces (Fact 1.4; "isogenies" in Huybrechts' sense), and rational Hodge isometries between $H^2$'s of projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type (Markman 2024): multiplier $1$.
2. The map induced on transcendental lattices by a twisted derived equivalence $D^{b}(S_1,\alpha_1)\cong D^{b}(S_2,\alpha_2)$ (Huybrechts–Stellari, Math. Ann. 332 (2005)): the equivalence induces a Hodge isometry of the twisted Mukai lattices $\widetilde H(S_i,B_i,\mathbb Z)$, which as lattices are the Mukai lattices $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Z)$ with the Hodge structure transported by the rational isometry $\exp(B_i)$ of $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Q)$; the transcendental part of $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Q)$ is $T(S_i)_{\mathbb Q}$. So the induced rational map $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge isometry: multiplier $1$. (This is exactly why twisted derived equivalence implies isogeny, Huybrechts 2019.)
3. The map $T(S)\to T(M)$ induced by a (twisted) universal family on a smooth projective moduli space $M=M_H(v)$ of stable (twisted) sheaves, $v$ primitive, $\dim M=2n\ge 2$: Mukai's Hodge isometry $v^{\perp}\cong H^2(M,\mathbb Z)$ (for $n\ge2$; $v^{\perp}/\mathbb Zv$ for $n=1$) restricts to an isometry $T(S)\cong T(M)$ (Mukai 1987; O'Grady 1997; Yoshioka 2001; twisted case Yoshioka 2006): multiplier $1$.
4. Pull-back along a dominant rational map $f:X\dashrightarrow Y$ of degree $\delta$ between projective K3 surfaces: multiplier $\delta$; between projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of dimension $2n$ with $\operatorname{rk}T(Y)\ge 3$: multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ with $\lambda^{n}=\delta\,c_Y/c_X$ (Lemma 3.3).
5. The adjoint (transpose) of a similitude of multiplier $\lambda$: multiplier $\lambda$ (if $\psi^{\dagger}\psi=\lambda$ and $\psi$ is invertible then $\psi\psi^{\dagger}=\psi(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi^{-1}=\lambda$).

**Lemma 3.3 (dominant rational maps).** Let $f:X\dashrightarrow Y$ be a dominant rational map of degree $\delta$ between projective K3 surfaces, or between projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of the same dimension $2n$ with $\operatorname{rk}T(Y)\ge3$. Then there is an algebraic morphism of Hodge structures $f^{*}:T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ (induced by the transpose of the closure of the graph of $f$) which is a Hodge similitude with multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, $\lambda=\delta$ for K3 surfaces and $\lambda^{n}=\delta c_Y/c_X$ in general.

*Proof.* Let $\pi:\tilde X\to X$ be a resolution of the indeterminacy of $f$ by blow-ups along smooth centres and $g=f\circ\pi:\tilde X\to Y$, a generically finite surjective morphism of degree $\delta$. Then $H^2(\tilde X,\mathbb Q)=\pi^{*}H^2(X,\mathbb Q)\oplus A$, where $A$ is spanned by classes of exceptional divisors (algebraic), $\pi_*\pi^{*}=\mathrm{id}$ and $\pi_*A=0$ (an exceptional divisor maps onto a subvariety of codimension $\ge 2$). The transcendental part of $H^2(\tilde X)$ is therefore $\pi^{*}T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$. $g^{*}$ is a morphism of Hodge structures, injective because $g_*g^{*}=\delta$; being a morphism out of the irreducible, non-$(1,1)$ Hodge structure $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, it maps $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ into the transcendental part, i.e. $g^{*}T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\subseteq\pi^{*}T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$. Put $f^{*}:=(\pi^{*})^{-1}g^{*}|_{T(Y)}$. With $\overline{\Gamma}_f:=(\pi,g)_*[\tilde X]\subset X\times Y$ one has $({}^t\overline{\Gamma}_f)_*=\pi_*g^{*}$, which agrees with $f^{*}$ on $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ (as $g^{*}x=\pi^{*}f^{*}x$ there): $f^{*}$ is algebraic. Multiplier: for $\alpha\in T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, $\int_X(f^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\int_{\tilde X}(\pi^{*}f^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\int_{\tilde X}(g^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\delta\int_Y\alpha^{2n}$ (projection formula, $g_*g^{*}=\delta$ on $H^{4n}$). For K3 surfaces ($n=1$) this says $(f^{*}\alpha,f^{*}\alpha)=\delta(\alpha,\alpha)$, and polarization gives multiplier $\delta$. In general the Fujiki relations give $c_X\,q_X(f^{*}\alpha)^{n}=\delta c_Y\,q_Y(\alpha)^{n}$ for all $\alpha\in T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, hence as polynomial functions on $T(Y)_{\mathbb C}$. Since $q_Y|_{T(Y)}$ is a non-degenerate quadratic form in $\ge 3$ variables it is an irreducible polynomial; by unique factorisation in $\mathbb C[T(Y)]$, $q_Y$ divides $q_X\circ f^{*}$, so $q_X\circ f^{*}=\lambda\,q_Y$ with $\lambda^{n}=\delta c_Y/c_X$; $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$ because both forms are defined over $\mathbb Q$ and $q_Y\ne0$, and $\lambda>0$ because both forms are positive on the planes $P$, with $f^{*}P_Y=P_X$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4 (what the strategy cannot do).** Let $S$ have RM by a field $E$ that is not multiquadratic. Then no element of $E\setminus E^{(2)}$ — for $[E:\mathbb Q]$ odd, no element of $E\setminus\mathbb Q$ — is a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites of maps of the five kinds listed in Lemma 3.2. In other words, *no combination of Buskin's theorem, Huybrechts' isogenies, twisted Fourier–Mukai partners, Mukai's moduli-space correspondences, Markman's theorem, finite covers and quotients, and their transposes can prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$.* Ladder step (a) in its literal form — "decompose $e\in E$ as a sum of products of rational Hodge isometries between $S$ and Fourier–Mukai partners" — is impossible for every $E\ne\mathbb Q$: products of isometries from $T(S)$ to $T(S)$ are $\pm 1$.

*Proof.* Each listed map is a Hodge similitude (Lemma 3.2), so every composite ending at $T(S)$ is the value of a similitude chain; apply Theorem A. For the last sentence: a Hodge isometry $T\to T$ is an element $e\in E$ with $e^{2}=1$. $\square$

**Remark 3.5 (non-vacuity).** Bayer-Fluckiger–van Geemen–Schütt (arXiv:2401.04072, 2024, Thm. A) prove: for every totally real field $E$ of degree $d$ and every $m\ge 3$ with $md\le 21$ there is an $(m-2)$-dimensional family of complex projective K3 surfaces with RM by $E$. Thus every totally real field of degree $2,\dots,7$ occurs; for $d\in\{3,5,7\}$ and for non-multiquadratic $E$ of degree $4$ or $6$ Theorem A applies with $E^{(2)}\subsetneq E$. Elsenhans–Jahnel (LMS J. Comput. Math. 2014; Michigan Math. J. 2023) give explicit families over $\mathbb Q$ with $\rho=16$ and RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ and by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$.

**Remark 3.6 (passing through $H^{4}$ of a moduli space does not help).** One might hope for correspondences whose action on $T$ factors through $H^{2k}(X)$, $k\ge2$, of a hyper-Kähler $X$ with $T(X)\cong T$, rather than through $T(X)$. For $X$ of $K3^{[2]}$-type, $H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q)=\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{2}(X,\mathbb Q)$ ($b_4=276=\binom{24}{2}$), and I claim $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(-1),\operatorname{Sym}^2H^2(X))=\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(-1),NS(X)\otimes T(X))$, i.e. every such map is $x\mapsto\sum_i D_i\cdot\phi_i(x)$ with $D_i\in NS(X)$ and $\phi_i\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T(X))$; dually for maps $\operatorname{Sym}^2H^2(X)(1)\to T$. Indeed, Hodge morphisms are the morphisms equivariant for the Mumford–Tate group, which in the RM case is $\operatorname{Res}_{E/\mathbb Q}SO(T,\psi)$ (Zarhin); over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, $T=\bigoplus_\sigma T_\sigma$ with $T_\sigma$ the standard representation of the $\sigma$-th factor $SO_m$, and $\operatorname{Sym}^2T=\bigoplus_\sigma(\mathbf 1\oplus\operatorname{Sym}^2_0T_\sigma)\oplus\bigoplus_{\sigma<\tau}T_\sigma\otimes T_\tau$, whose irreducible constituents have dimensions $1$, $\frac{m(m+1)}2-1>m$, $m^{2}>m$ for $m\ge3$, so none is isomorphic to a $T_\rho$; the summands $\operatorname{Sym}^2NS$ are of type $(2,2)$. Hence the composite $T\to H^4(X)(1)\to T$ of two such maps is $\sum_{i}\ell(D_i)\,\chi\circ\phi_i$ with $\chi,\phi_i\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}$ between transcendental lattices — and if $\chi,\phi_i$ are produced by the constructions of Lemma 3.2 they are similitudes and Theorem A applies again. (I have not checked the analogous statement for other deformation types or for $H^{2k}$, $k\ge3$; it is not needed for Theorem A, which is a statement about similitudes.)

## 4. The real quadratic case: an exact reduction, and what is actually known

Let $\Lambda(S)\subseteq\mathbb Q_{>0}/\mathbb Q^{*2}$ be the set of classes of multipliers $\lambda$ for which there exist a projective K3 surface $Y$ and an **algebraic** Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier $\lambda$. Clearly $1\in\Lambda(S)$ (the identity) and $\Lambda(S)$ depends only on the isogeny class of $S$ (compose with Buskin's isometries).

**Proposition 4.1 (the quadratic part of $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$.
1. If $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$, then *every* Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda$ from $T$ to the transcendental lattice of *any* projective K3 surface is algebraic.
2. For $a\in E$ with $a^{2}=\lambda\in\mathbb Q$: $\;a\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}\iff\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$.
3. Consequently $E^{\mathrm{alg}}\supseteq E^{(2)}$ if and only if $\Lambda(S)\supseteq\{\lambda:\sqrt\lambda\in E\}$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $\psi:T\to T_Y$ be algebraic with $N(\psi)=\lambda$ and $\phi:T\to T_Z$ any Hodge similitude with $N(\phi)=\lambda$. Then $\phi\psi^{-1}=\lambda^{-1}\phi\psi^{\dagger}:T_Y\to T_Z$ satisfies $N(\phi\psi^{-1})=\lambda^{-2}\,\psi\,\phi^{\dagger}\phi\,\psi^{\dagger}=\lambda^{-2}\cdot\lambda\cdot\psi\psi^{\dagger}=1$ (Lemma 3.2(5)), so it is a rational Hodge isometry between transcendental lattices of projective K3 surfaces, algebraic by Fact 1.4; hence $\phi=(\phi\psi^{-1})\circ\psi$ is algebraic. (2) "$\Rightarrow$": $a$ is itself an algebraic Hodge similitude $T\to T$ of multiplier $a^{2}=\lambda$ (Lemma 2.2(2)). "$\Leftarrow$": let $\psi:T\to T_Y$ be algebraic with $N(\psi)=\lambda$. Then $\theta:=\psi\circ a^{-1}:T\to T_Y$ is a morphism of Hodge structures with $N(\theta)=a^{-2}\lambda=1$, i.e. a rational Hodge isometry, algebraic by Fact 1.4; so $a=\theta^{-1}\psi=\theta^{\dagger}\psi$ is algebraic (Lemma 1.5). (3) $E^{(2)}$ is generated by the $a$ with $a^{2}\in\mathbb Q$, and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a field (Lemma 1.3). $\square$

**Theorem C (real quadratic RM: exact reduction).** Let $S$ have RM by $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$, $D>1$ square-free. The following are equivalent:
1. the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$;
2. $\sqrt D\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$;
3. $D\in\Lambda(S)$: there exist a projective K3 surface $Y$ and an algebraic Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier $Dc^{2}$, $c\in\mathbb Q^{*}$;
4. for every projective K3 surface $Y$, every Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier in $D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$ is algebraic;
5. the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times Y$ for one (equivalently, every) projective K3 surface $Y$ isogenous to $S$.

*Proof.* (1)$\Leftrightarrow$(2): $E=\mathbb Q\oplus\mathbb Q\sqrt D$, $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a field (Lemma 1.3). (2)$\Leftrightarrow$(3): Proposition 4.1(2) with $a=\sqrt D$. (3)$\Rightarrow$(4): Proposition 4.1(1). (4)$\Rightarrow$(3): apply (4) to $Y=S$ and the similitude $\sqrt D$. (1)$\Leftrightarrow$(5): for $Y$ isogenous to $S$ fix an algebraic Hodge isometry $\theta:T\to T_Y$; the Hodge classes on $S\times Y$ are $\mathbb Q^{2}\oplus NS_S\otimes NS_Y\oplus\{\text{classes inducing }\theta e,\ e\in E\}$ (same proof as Lemma 1.3), and $\theta e$ is algebraic iff $e=\theta^{\dagger}(\theta e)$ is. $\square$

**Theorem C′ (a sufficient condition).** Let $S$ have RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$. Suppose there are a projective K3 surface $S'$ isogenous to $S$ (for instance $S'=S$, or any twisted Fourier–Mukai partner of $S$), a projective K3 surface $S''$, and a dominant rational map $S'\dashrightarrow S''$ or $S''\dashrightarrow S'$ of degree $\delta\in D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$. Then the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$.

*Proof.* Lemma 3.3 gives an algebraic Hodge similitude of multiplier $\delta$ between $T_{S'}$ and $T_{S''}$, in one direction; its adjoint is algebraic (Lemma 1.5) with the same multiplier (Lemma 3.2(5)), so there is an algebraic similitude $T_{S'}\to T_{S''}$ of multiplier $\delta$. Composing with an algebraic Hodge isometry $T\to T_{S'}$ (Fact 1.4) gives $\delta\in\Lambda(S)$; apply Theorem C, (3)$\Rightarrow$(1). $\square$

**Corollary 4.2 (Varesco's theorem, and its reach).** (a) If a K3 surface isogenous to $S$ carries a symplectic automorphism of order $p\in\{2,3\}$ and $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p)$, then the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$. This is Varesco's theorem (Math. Z. 2023, arXiv:2304.02519, Thm. 2.1 / 5.3); his proof is the same mechanism: the quotient map is an algebraic similitude of multiplier $p$, and $\sqrt p$ is recovered by composing with a Buskin isometry. (b) More generally, if a K3 surface $S'$ isogenous to $S$ carries a faithful symplectic action of a finite group $G$ with $|G|\in D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$, the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ with $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$: the quotient $S'\dashrightarrow\widetilde{S'/G}$ has degree $|G|$ and $\widetilde{S'/G}$ is a K3 surface (Nikulin 1980). (c) In the RM range $\rho\le16$ only $|G|$ with prime factors $2,3$ occur: a symplectic automorphism of order $5$ (resp. $7$) has coinvariant lattice of rank $16$ (resp. $18$) (Nikulin 1980), forcing $\rho\ge17$ (resp. $\ge19$); hence (b) can only ever produce $D\in\{2,3,6\}$. The existence constraints are $\rho\ge 9$ for $\mathbb Z/2$ (coinvariant lattice $E_8(-2)$ of rank $8$; conversely a primitive embedding $E_8(-2)\hookrightarrow NS(S')$ gives a Nikulin involution, van Geemen–Sarti 2007), $\rho\ge13$ for $\mathbb Z/3$ (rank $12$), and $\rho\ge15$ for $S_3$ (rank $14$: $\operatorname{rk}H^2(S)^{S_3}=\frac16(22+3\cdot6+2\cdot4)=8$ by the Lefschetz fixed point formula, a symplectic involution having $8$ fixed points and an element of order $3$ having $6$); with $d=2$ the Picard number is even, so $\rho\in\{10,12,14,16\}$, $\{14,16\}$, $\{16\}$ respectively. (d) Theorem C′ does **not** require the map to be Galois. A dominant rational map of degree $5$ (or $5c^{2}$) between K3 surfaces of Picard number $16$ would settle the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ for Elsenhans–Jahnel's explicit $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$-family; I know of no such map, and no symplectic group action can provide one (c). This is a precise, concrete open sub-problem.

**Remark 4.3 (the problem lives inside the isogeny class).** For RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$, a K3 surface $Y$ is Hodge-similar to $S$ with multiplier $D$ iff it is isogenous to $S$: if $N(\phi)=D$ then $N(\phi\circ\sqrt D^{-1})=D^{-1}\cdot D=1$, and conversely $N(\theta\sqrt D)=D$. So all the surfaces $S''$ in Theorem C′ are automatically isogenous to $S$ (hence twisted Fourier–Mukai partners of $S$ by Huybrechts 2019), the Nikulin quotient of an RM-by-$\sqrt2$ surface is isogenous to it, and the whole real quadratic problem is a statement about the single derived-equivalence class of $S$: *is the self-similitude $\sqrt D$ of $T$ algebraic?* Buskin's theorem says that the isometries inside this class are algebraic; nothing known says anything about the similitudes of non-square multiplier, except when a finite map supplies one.

## 5. Twists $S_q$, and the shape of the general problem

**Proposition 5.1 (existence of twists).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$ with $\rho(S)\ge3$ (i.e. $r=\operatorname{rk}T\le19$), and let $q\in E$ be totally positive. Then there exist a projective K3 surface $S_q$ and an isomorphism of Hodge structures $\phi_q:T\to T_{S_q}$ with $N(\phi_q)=q$, i.e. $(\phi_qx,\phi_qy)_{S_q}=(x,qy)$ for all $x,y\in T$. Any two such surfaces are isogenous; $S_q$ is isogenous to $S$ iff $q\in E^{*2}$, and Hodge-similar to $S$ iff $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\cdot E^{*2}$.

*Proof.* (i) The form $(x,y)_q:=(x,qy)$ on $T$ is symmetric ($q^{\dagger}=q$), non-degenerate, and on $T_\sigma$ equals $\sigma(q)(\,,)$ with $\sigma(q)>0$; hence it has signature $(2,r-2)$, is positive on $P$, negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$, and for $\omega$ spanning $H^{2,0}$ one has $(\omega,\omega)_q=\sigma_1(q)(\omega,\omega)=0$ and $(\omega,\bar\omega)_q=\sigma_1(q)(\omega,\bar\omega)>0$ (the $\mathbb C$-bilinear extension; $q\omega=\sigma_1(q)\omega$ by Fact 1.2).
(ii) The rational quadratic space $V=(T,(\,,)_q)$, of dimension $r\le19$ and signature $(2,r-2)$, embeds isometrically into $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}:=\Lambda_{K3}\otimes\mathbb Q$ (signature $(3,19)$). Indeed, by the classification of quadratic forms over $\mathbb Q$ (Serre, *A course in arithmetic*, Ch. IV, Prop. 6, Prop. 7, Thm. 9) a quadratic space $V'$ over $\mathbb Q$ of dimension $22-r\ge3$ exists with the local invariants $d(V'_v)=d(\Lambda)d(V)$, $\varepsilon_v(V')=\varepsilon_v(\Lambda)\varepsilon_v(V)\,(d(V),d(V'))_v$ for all places $v$ and signature $(1,21-r)$: these data are consistent at $\infty$ (they are the invariants of the real orthogonal complement), satisfy the product formula (because $\Lambda$, $V$ and the Hilbert symbol do), and in dimension $\ge3$ every pair $(d,\varepsilon_v)$ is realised locally. Then $V\oplus V'$ and $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$ have the same dimension, discriminant, signature and Hasse invariants, hence are isometric (Hasse–Minkowski), which gives $\iota:V\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$.
(iii) $L:=\iota(V)\cap\Lambda_{K3}$ is a primitive sublattice, even (as a sublattice of the even lattice $\Lambda_{K3}$), with $L_{\mathbb Q}=\iota(V)$. The vector $\omega':=\iota(\omega)\in L_{\mathbb C}$ satisfies $(\omega',\omega')=0$, $(\omega',\bar\omega')>0$. By the surjectivity of the period map for K3 surfaces (Todorov 1980, Looijenga 1981; Huybrechts, *Lectures on K3 surfaces*, Ch. 7, Thm. 4.1) there is a K3 surface $S_q$ with a marking $H^2(S_q,\mathbb Z)\cong\Lambda_{K3}$ under which $H^{2,0}(S_q)=\mathbb C\omega'$.
(iv) $NS(S_q)=\omega'^{\perp}\cap\Lambda_{K3}\supseteq L^{\perp}$, which has signature $(1,21-r)$; so $NS(S_q)$ contains a class $h$ with $h^{2}>0$, and $S_q$ is projective (Riemann–Roch gives $h^0(h)+h^0(-h)\ge2+h^{2}/2$, so $\pm h$ is effective and big, and a Kähler surface with a big line bundle is projective).
(v) $L_{\mathbb Q}$ is a sub-Hodge structure of $H^2(S_q,\mathbb Q)$: it contains $\omega'$ and $\bar\omega'$, on whose span the form is non-degenerate, so $L_{\mathbb C}=\langle\omega',\bar\omega'\rangle\oplus(L_{\mathbb C}\cap\langle\omega',\bar\omega'\rangle^{\perp})$ is the induced Hodge decomposition. Moreover $\iota:T\to L_{\mathbb Q}$ is an isomorphism of Hodge structures: both sides have $H^{2,0}$ spanned by $\omega$ resp. $\omega'$, and the $(1,1)$-part of $T$ is the orthogonal of $\{\omega,\bar\omega\}$ for $(\,,)$, which coincides with the orthogonal for $(\,,)_q$ because $q\omega=\sigma_1(q)\omega$. Hence $L_{\mathbb Q}$ is irreducible and contains $H^{2,0}(S_q)$; since $T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}$ is the smallest sub-Hodge structure containing $H^{2,0}(S_q)$ and is contained in $L_{\mathbb Q}$, $T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}=L_{\mathbb Q}$ and $\phi_q:=\iota$ is a Hodge isometry $(T,(\,,)_q)\to T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}$, i.e. $N(\phi_q)=q$.
(vi) Uniqueness up to isogeny: if $\phi'_q:T\to T_{S'_q}$ is another such map, $\phi'_q\phi_q^{-1}$ is a Hodge isometry. By Lemma 2.2(1) every $\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is $\phi_qa$ with $N(\phi)=qa^{2}$; so an isometry (resp. similitude) exists iff $q\in E^{*2}$ (resp. $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}E^{*2}$). $\square$

**Proposition 5.2 (what the twists would give, and why they are out of reach).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$, $\rho(S)\ge3$.
1. If for some totally positive $q$ some $\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is algebraic, then $E^{\mathrm{alg}}\supseteq\mathbb Q(N(\phi))$ with $N(\phi)\in q\cdot E^{*2}$.
2. The Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ **iff** there are totally positive $q_1,\dots,q_k\in E$ and algebraic $\phi_i\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_{q_i}})$ with $\mathbb Q\big(N(\phi_1),\dots,N(\phi_k)\big)=E$.
3. If $q\notin\mathbb Q_{>0}\cdot E^{*2}$, no element of $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is a Hodge similitude; hence none of the constructions of Lemma 3.2, nor any composite of them, produces an algebraic class in $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$. If $q=\lambda a^{2}$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$, then $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ contains an algebraic element iff $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$, and then all of $\phi_qE^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is algebraic.
4. If $E$ is not multiquadratic, the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ **requires** an algebraic morphism of Hodge structures $T\to T_Y$ (some projective K3 surface $Y$) which is not a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites of similitudes. No such morphism is known for any K3 surface with real multiplication by any field.

*Proof.* (1) Lemma 2.2(4),(5) and Lemma 1.3. (2) "$\Leftarrow$": Lemma 2.2(5). "$\Rightarrow$": take $a\in E$ with $\mathbb Q(a)=E$, $q_1=a^{2}$, $q_2=(a+1)^{2}$, $S_{q_i}=S$ (with $\phi_{q_1}=a$, $\phi_{q_2}=a+1$, which are allowed by Lemma 2.2(1),(2)); they are algebraic by hypothesis and $\mathbb Q(a^{2},(a+1)^{2})\ni 2a$. (3) By Proposition 5.1 every element of $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ has norm in $qE^{*2}$, which meets $\mathbb Q$ iff $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}E^{*2}$; a composite of similitudes ending at $T_{S_q}$ is a similitude. For the second sentence use Proposition 4.1(1) and Lemma 2.2(1). (4) If $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E\ne E^{(2)}$, pick $e\in E\setminus E^{(2)}$: it is algebraic, and by Theorem A it is not in $\mathcal S(S)$; the last sentence is a literature statement (Lemma 3.2 is, to my knowledge, a complete inventory of the available constructions between K3 surfaces and hyper-Kähler manifolds; the only known construction that leaves this world, the Kuga–Satake correspondence, is not known to be algebraic for any K3 surface with $\rho\le16$ outside Paranjape's and Varesco–Voisin's special $\rho=16$ families). $\square$

**Remark 5.3 (on the "exact reduction" requested in ladder step (c)).** (a) *Brauer classes / twisted sheaves.* Lemma 3.2(2),(3) and Theorem A show that no statement about Brauer classes, twisted sheaves, or moduli spaces of (twisted) sheaves on K3 surfaces or hyper-Kähler manifolds can be *equivalent* to the algebraicity of the RM classes when $E$ is not multiquadratic: everything such a statement yields on transcendental lattices is a similitude. For $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ the exact reduction is Theorem C: the RM classes are algebraic iff the similitudes of multiplier $D$ out of $T$ are algebraic. (b) *Kuga–Satake.* If the Kuga–Satake correspondence $T\hookrightarrow H^2(A\times A)$ of $S$ is algebraic, then $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$ (van Geemen 2008 computes $\operatorname{End}^0$ of the Kuga–Satake variety of an RM surface, and $E$ acts on it by endomorphisms; Schlickewei 2010, Thm. 2, carries this out for the $\rho=16$ six-line family, where Paranjape proved the Kuga–Satake correspondence algebraic; Varesco 2023 proves more generally that the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture for $X,Y$ makes every Hodge similitude $T_X\to T_Y$ algebraic). I do not know a converse, and I do not claim one: the implication "RM classes algebraic $\Rightarrow$ Kuga–Satake algebraic" would be a statement about the abelian variety $A$ that the K3 surface alone does not control. So the honest content of (c) is: for real quadratic $E$ an exact reduction to a similitude statement (Theorem C); for non-multiquadratic $E$ a proof that no reduction to Brauer-class or similitude statements exists (Theorem A), and a one-way reduction to Kuga–Satake.

## 6. GAPS (explicit)

**G1 — the top target.** *Needed:* for every projective K3 surface $S$ with RM by $E$, $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$. *Why it is needed:* Lemma 1.3 — it is exactly the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$. *Why I cannot prove it:* Theorem A. For non-multiquadratic $E$ (all odd degrees, most quartic and sextic fields) every algebraic correspondence that this strategy knows how to build acts on $T$ as a Hodge similitude, and $\mathbb Q$-combinations of composites of similitudes never leave $E^{(2)}\subsetneq E$. A proof must exhibit an algebraic morphism $T\to T_Y$ with $N(\phi)\notin E^{(2)}$ — for instance to a twist $S_q$ with $q\notin\mathbb Q E^{*2}$ (Proposition 5.2) — and no mechanism in the K3/hyper-Kähler world (isometries, (twisted) derived equivalences, moduli spaces, finite maps, higher cohomology of $K3^{[2]}$-type spaces) produces one. The only known mechanism leaving this world, the Kuga–Satake correspondence, is not known to be algebraic for any RM surface except in the special $\rho=16$ families of Paranjape and Varesco–Voisin (there the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ is known, Schlickewei 2010).

**G2 — the real quadratic case in general.** *Needed:* $D\in\Lambda(S)$, i.e. one algebraic Hodge similitude of multiplier $Dc^{2}$ out of $T$ (Theorem C). Equivalently (Proposition 4.1): "every rational Hodge similitude of multiplier $D$ between transcendental lattices of projective K3 surfaces is algebraic" — a 'Buskin theorem for similitudes', which is the natural conjecture here (it follows from the Hodge conjecture; Varesco shows it follows from the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture). *Why I cannot prove it:* Buskin's and Huybrechts' proofs run through (twisted) Fourier–Mukai equivalences, and every exact equivalence of (twisted) derived categories of K3 surfaces induces an *isometry* of Mukai lattices (Mukai, Orlov, Huybrechts–Stellari), so no categorical construction can realise a non-square multiplier; the only geometric sources of non-square multipliers are dominant rational maps (Lemma 3.3), whose existence imposes lattice conditions on $NS$ ($E_8(-2)\subset NS$ etc.) that most RM surfaces do not satisfy, and which in the RM range can only give $D\in\{2,3,6\}$ via group actions (Corollary 4.2(c)). Concretely open: $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ (Elsenhans–Jahnel's explicit $\rho=16$ family), $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ with $\rho\le8$ (no Nikulin involution possible on any isogenous surface, since isogenous surfaces have the same $\rho$ and a Nikulin involution or quotient forces $\rho\ge9$), and all $D\notin\{2,3,6\}$ modulo squares. A dominant rational map of degree $5c^{2}$ between K3 surfaces of Picard number $16$ would close the $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ case (Theorem C′); I do not know whether such maps exist.

**G3 — existence of examples for Corollary 4.2(b) with $D=6$.** I have not verified that K3 surfaces with RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$ and a symplectic $S_3$-action exist (this needs: an even lattice $NS$ of rank $16$ containing the rank-$14$ coinvariant lattice of $S_3$, whose orthogonal complement $T$ of rank $6$ is rationally the transfer $\operatorname{tr}_{E/\mathbb Q}\circ\psi$ of a ternary form $\psi$ over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$ of signatures $(2,1)$ and $(0,3)$ at the two real places; then van Geemen's existence theorem for RM Hodge structures on $T$). For $D=2,3$ the families are provided by Varesco. Corollary 4.2(b) is a correct implication but may be vacuous for $D=6$.

**G4 — literature items used but not re-proved.** Buskin's theorem (Fact 1.4) and its transcendental corollary; Zarhin's theorem (Fact 1.1) and, in Remark 3.6 only, Zarhin's description of the Mumford–Tate group; Huybrechts–Stellari's twisted Mukai-lattice isometry and Mukai/O'Grady/Yoshioka's $v^{\perp}\cong H^2(M)$ (Lemma 3.2(2),(3), used only in the inventory, i.e. in the negative Corollary 3.4); Markman's theorem (inventory only); Nikulin's results on symplectic automorphisms (Corollary 4.2); the surjectivity of the period map and Hasse–Minkowski (Proposition 5.1); Bayer-Fluckiger–van Geemen–Schütt 2024 (non-vacuity of Theorem A, Remark 3.5). The exact statements were checked against the abstracts and secondary sources available (arXiv itself is blocked here); I could not read Varesco's paper in full, so the attribution "Theorem 2.1 / 5.3" is from the abstract and search snippets, and the published version may state the symplectic-automorphism theorem only for $p=2,3$ (which, by Corollary 4.2(c), is anyway the only non-vacuous range).

**G5 — the Kuga–Satake implication (Remark 5.3(b))** is quoted, not proved, and no theorem above depends on it.

## 7. Checklist (docs/03, §3.4)

**Where projectivity is essential.** (i) Fact 1.4 (Buskin) is proved with moduli spaces of stable twisted sheaves on *projective* K3 surfaces; for non-projective K3 surfaces Buskin only obtains polynomials in Chern classes of coherent analytic sheaves, which is exactly the formulation in which the Kähler Hodge conjecture fails (Voisin 2002). Every positive statement here that produces a cycle — Proposition 4.1, Theorems C and C′, Proposition 5.2(3) — goes through Fact 1.4, and therefore through projectivity. (ii) Theorem C′ needs the closure of the graph of a rational map to a *projective* K3 surface as an algebraic cycle, and quotients $\widetilde{S'/G}$ of projective surfaces are projective. (iii) Proposition 5.1 produces a *projective* $S_q$ precisely because $L^{\perp}$ has signature $(1,21-r)$, i.e. contains a class of positive square; a non-projective twist would be useless. (iv) Lemma 1.5(b) uses an ample class. (v) Theorem A and the facts of §1–2 are pure Hodge theory and hold for Kähler K3 surfaces; they are *negative* statements and prove nothing false. Would the argument "prove" the Kähler Hodge conjecture? No: without projectivity there is no Fact 1.4 and no graph cycle, and the only output of the method for a non-projective K3 is analytic Chern classes.

**Where rational coefficients are essential.** (i) $E$, $\sqrt D$, $\phi_q$ and all norms live on $T\otimes\mathbb Q$; the order $\{e\in E:eT(S)\subseteq T(S)\}$ acts integrally but nothing integral is claimed or used. (ii) Buskin's classes $Z_\phi$ are rational cycles; the projectors $\pi_{NS},\pi_T$ of Lemma 1.3 have denominators (the inverse Gram matrix of $NS$); the adjoint correspondence of Lemma 1.5(b) carries the factor $(2n-1)/(c_Yq_Y(h)^{n-1})$; Proposition 4.1 divides by $\lambda$ and by $a$; the CM argument of Remark 2.3 divides by $a^{2}+\delta$. The output of Theorem C′ is "$N\cdot\gamma_{\sqrt D}$ is the class of an integral cycle for some unknown $N\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$". Would the argument "prove" the integral Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$? No: it produces multiples only, with uncontrolled $N$, as C1 of docs/03 demands.

**Does the argument prove too much?** Applied to $E=\mathbb Q$ it is empty; applied to CM surfaces it reproduces the known theorem (Remark 2.3); applied to RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ with a Nikulin involution it reproduces Varesco's theorem; it never manufactures a cycle from Hodge theory alone — every cycle is a Buskin class, a graph, a diagonal, or a product of divisors. The negative results (Theorem A, Corollary 3.4, Proposition 5.2(3),(4)) are consistent with the known cases: in the CM case the isometries span $E$ (Remark 2.3), in the RM case they span $\mathbb Q$ — this is precisely why the CM case is a theorem and the RM case is open.

**Other items of §3.4.** Absoluteness: the classes $\gamma_e$ are absolute Hodge (Deligne 1982 via Kuga–Satake; André), consistent but not used. Lefschetz $B$ for $S\times S$ holds (products of surfaces). Variational Hodge conjecture: not touched; note that the locus of RM surfaces in moduli is an $(m-2)$-dimensional family along which $\gamma_e$ stays Hodge, so G1 is a (hard) instance of it.
