**Verdict in one paragraph.** The top target is **not** proved: every element of $E=\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})$, with $E$ totally real of degree $>1$, is algebraic on $S\times S$. What is proved, with complete proofs, is elementary and essentially known.

**(A) The $H^2$-level similitude obstruction (Theorem A).** Take all composites $T(S)\to T(X_1)\to\dots\to T(S)$ of Hodge similitudes between transcendental lattices of K3 surfaces and hyper-Kähler manifolds. Their $\mathbb Q$-span is exactly the multiquadratic field $E^{(2)}$ generated by the $e\in E$ with $e^2\in\mathbb Q$. If only algebraic similitudes are allowed, the span is exactly $E^{\mathrm{alg}}\cap E^{(2)}$.

The following all act on transcendental lattices in degree 2 as similitudes (Lemma 3.2): rational Hodge isometries (Buskin, Markman), twisted derived equivalences, Mukai's moduli maps and dominant rational maps. So these maps, composed at the level of $H^2$, never reach $E\setminus E^{(2)}$. When $[E:\mathbb Q]$ is odd they reach only $\mathbb Q$. In substance this is van Geemen 2008, §7.9, and Varesco 2023, Rem. 2.2.

It is **not** an obstruction to the strategy as a whole. Remark 3.6 gives a correspondence through $H^6$ of a hyper-Kähler fourfold that realises every $e\in E$, as soon as one Hodge class in its $H^4$ is algebraic.

**(B) The real quadratic case (Theorem C).** For $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$, the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ holds iff there is one algebraic Hodge similitude of multiplier $D$ out of $T(S)$. Theorem C′ gives a sufficient condition that does not need a Galois cover. This repackages Varesco 2023, Thm. 2.1.

**(C) Real twists (Proposition 5.1).** For every totally positive $q\in E$ and $\rho(S)\ge 3$ there is a real twist $S_q$. This is van Geemen's real twist (2008, §4.7, stated there for $\dim T\le 11$), extended routinely. The corrected Proposition 5.2 describes the algebraic maps $T(S)\to T(S_q)$.

Nothing here settles an open case. Gaps: §6. Attempt and failure: §7. Response to referees: §8. Checklist: §9. Claimed results: final table.

## 1. Setting, notation, standing facts

Throughout, $S$ is a complex projective K3 surface, $H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\cong\Lambda_{K3}=U^{3}\oplus E_8(-1)^{2}$ with the intersection form $(\,,)$, $NS(S)=H^2(S,\mathbb Z)\cap H^{1,1}(S)$, $\rho=\operatorname{rk}NS(S)\ge 1$, $T(S)=NS(S)^{\perp}$ the transcendental lattice, $r=\operatorname{rk}T(S)=22-\rho$. Write $T=T(S)_{\mathbb Q}$: a rational Hodge structure of weight 2 with $h^{2,0}=1$, polarized by $(\,,)$, which has signature $(2,r-2)$ on $T_{\mathbb R}$, is positive definite on the real 2-plane $P=T_{\mathbb R}\cap(H^{2,0}\oplus H^{0,2})$ and negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}=P^{\perp}$. "Algebraic" always means: in the image of the cycle class map with $\mathbb Q$-coefficients. For a correspondence $\Gamma\in CH^{*}(X\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ we write $\Gamma_*$ for its action on cohomology and ${}^{t}\Gamma$ for its transpose; composites of algebraic correspondences are algebraic and act by the composite map (Fulton, *Intersection theory*, Ch. 16).

**Fact 1.1 (Zarhin 1983).** $T$ is an irreducible Hodge structure; $E:=\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)$ is a number field; the adjoint involution $e\mapsto e^{\dagger}$, defined by $(ex,y)=(x,e^{\dagger}y)$, is either the identity — then $E$ is totally real (*real multiplication*, RM) — or non-trivial — then $E$ is a CM field and $\dagger$ is complex conjugation (*complex multiplication*, CM). (Zarhin, *Hodge groups of K3 surfaces*, Crelle 341 (1983), Thm. 1.5.1 and §1.6; Huybrechts, *Lectures on K3 surfaces*, Ch. 3.3.)

**Fact 1.2 (RM: decomposition at the real places, and $m\ge 3$).** Assume RM; put $d=[E:\mathbb Q]$, $m=\dim_E T$, so $r=md$. Over $\mathbb R$, $T_{\mathbb R}=\bigoplus_{\sigma:E\hookrightarrow\mathbb R}T_\sigma$, $T_\sigma=T\otimes_{E,\sigma}\mathbb R$, and the summands are mutually orthogonal: for $x\in T_\sigma$, $y\in T_\tau$ and $e\in E$, $\sigma(e)(x,y)=(ex,y)=(x,ey)=\tau(e)(x,y)$, so $(x,y)=0$ if $\sigma\ne\tau$. The line $H^{2,0}$ is an $E$-eigenline on which $E$ acts through one embedding $\sigma_1$ (the eigenvalue is real because $E$ is totally real), and $H^{0,2}=\overline{H^{2,0}}$ has the same eigenvalue; hence $P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$. Consequently $(\,,)|_{T_{\sigma_1}}$ has signature $(2,m-2)$, and $(\,,)|_{T_\sigma}$ is negative definite for $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$. Each $T_\sigma$ is of type $(1,1)$ for $\sigma\neq\sigma_1$. Finally $m\ge 3$ (van Geemen, Michigan Math. J. 56 (2008), Lemma 3.2). *Proof for completeness.* $m\ge 2$ since $P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$. Suppose $m=2$. Let $\psi:T\times T\to E$ be the $E$-bilinear form with $\operatorname{tr}_{E/\mathbb Q}\circ\psi=(\,,)$ (it exists and is symmetric because every $e\in E$ is self-adjoint). Its discriminant $\delta\in E^{*}/E^{*2}$ is totally positive ($\psi_{\sigma_1}$ is positive definite on $T_{\sigma_1}=P$, $\psi_\sigma$ negative definite for $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$), so $L:=E(\sqrt{-\delta})$ is a CM field and $(T,\psi)\cong(L,\,a\cdot N_{L/E})$ for some $a\in E^{*}$. The Hodge structure is a homomorphism $h:\mathbb C^{*}\to SO(T_{\mathbb R})$ which acts on $T_{\sigma_1}=P$ through $SO(P)=SO(\psi_{\sigma_1})$ and trivially on the other $T_\sigma$; each $SO(\psi_\sigma)$ is the group of norm-one elements of $L_\sigma=L\otimes_{E,\sigma}\mathbb R\cong\mathbb C$ acting by multiplication, which commutes with multiplication by $L$. So $L\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)=E$, contradicting $[L:E]=2$. $\square$

Thus RM forces $r=md\ge 6$, $\rho\le 16$, $d\le 7$; in particular no K3 surface with $\rho\ge 17$ (the Shioda–Inose / Morrison range where the Kuga–Satake correspondence is known to be algebraic) has RM.

**Lemma 1.3 (Hodge classes on $S\times S$).** $\operatorname{Hdg}^2(S\times S)=\mathbb Q[S\times\mathrm{pt}]\oplus\mathbb Q[\mathrm{pt}\times S]\oplus(NS(S)_{\mathbb Q}\otimes NS(S)_{\mathbb Q})\oplus\{\gamma_e: e\in E\}$, where $\gamma_e\in T\otimes T\subset H^4(S\times S,\mathbb Q)$ is the unique class with $(\gamma_e)_*|_{T}=e$ and $(\gamma_e)_*|_{NS}=0$. The first three summands are algebraic. Hence, with
$$E^{\mathrm{alg}}:=\{e\in E:\ \gamma_e \text{ is algebraic}\}=\{e\in E:\ e=\Gamma_*|_{T}\text{ for some algebraic }\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times S)_{\mathbb Q}\},$$
the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ (in all codimensions) iff $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$; and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a subfield of $E$.

*Proof.* Since $H^{\mathrm{odd}}(S)=0$, $H^4(S\times S)=H^0\otimes H^4\oplus H^2\otimes H^2\oplus H^4\otimes H^0$; the outer summands are spanned by $[\mathrm{pt}\times S]$, $[S\times\mathrm{pt}]$. The map $\gamma\mapsto\gamma_*=p_{2*}(\gamma\cup p_1^{*}(\cdot))$ is an isomorphism of Hodge structures $H^2\otimes H^2\cong\operatorname{End}(H^2(S,\mathbb Q))(-2)$, so Hodge classes in $H^2\otimes H^2$ correspond to $\operatorname{End}_{HS}(H^2(S,\mathbb Q))=\operatorname{End}(NS_{\mathbb Q})\oplus E$ (there are no non-zero Hodge morphisms between $NS_{\mathbb Q}$, a sum of copies of $\mathbb Q(-1)$, and the irreducible $T$ with $h^{2,0}\ne0$). Under this correspondence $NS\otimes NS\mapsto\operatorname{End}(NS)$ and $T\otimes T\mapsto\operatorname{End}(T)$. Products of divisors $p_1^{*}D\cdot p_2^{*}D'$ span $NS\otimes NS$. The projector $\pi_{NS}=\sum_{i,j}g^{ij}\,p_1^{*}D_i\cdot p_2^{*}D_j$ ($D_i$ a basis of $NS_{\mathbb Q}$, $(g^{ij})$ the inverse Gram matrix) is algebraic and $(\pi_{NS})_*$ is the orthogonal projection onto $NS_{\mathbb Q}$; hence $\pi_T:=\Delta-\pi_{NS}-[\mathrm{pt}\times S]-[S\times\mathrm{pt}]$ is algebraic with $(\pi_T)_*=$ projection onto $T$. If $\Gamma$ is algebraic with $\Gamma_*|_T=e$ then $\gamma_e=\pi_T\circ\Gamma\circ\pi_T$ is algebraic; the converse is trivial. Codimension-1 and codimension-3 Hodge classes on the fourfold $S\times S$ are algebraic by Lefschetz (1,1) and hard Lefschetz. Finally $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ contains $1$ (the diagonal), is a $\mathbb Q$-subspace, and is closed under composition (composition of correspondences), so it is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb Q$-subalgebra of the field $E$, i.e. a subfield. $\square$

**Fact 1.4 (Buskin; Huybrechts).** For projective K3 surfaces $S_1,S_2$, every Hodge isometry $\phi:H^2(S_1,\mathbb Q)\to H^2(S_2,\mathbb Q)$ is induced by an algebraic class $Z_\phi\in H^{2,2}(S_1\times S_2,\mathbb Q)$ (Buskin, Crelle 755 (2019), Thm. 1.1; second proof Huybrechts, Comment. Math. Helv. 94 (2019), Cor. 0.4). Consequently every Hodge isometry $\psi:T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ is algebraic: by Witt cancellation ($H^2(S_i,\mathbb Q)\cong\Lambda_{K3}\otimes\mathbb Q$ for both $i$) there is an isometry $NS(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\cong NS(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$, so $\psi$ extends to a Hodge isometry $\tilde\psi$ of $H^2$'s, and $\pi_{T_2}\circ Z_{\tilde\psi}\circ\pi_{T_1}$ is algebraic and induces $\psi$ (this corollary is stated in Buskin's paper). Earlier cases: Mukai (1987) for $\rho\ge 11$, extended by Nikulin (Math. USSR Izv. 30 (1988)) to $\rho\ge 5$ (as recalled in Schlickewei 2010, §1). *(Attribution corrected in round 2.)*

**Lemma 1.5 (adjoints are algebraic).** (a) Let $S,Y$ be projective K3 surfaces and $\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$. Then $({}^t\Gamma)_*:H^2(Y)\to H^2(S)$ is the adjoint of $\Gamma_*$ for the intersection pairings: $(\Gamma_*x,y)_Y=(x,({}^t\Gamma)_*y)_S$. (b) Let $Y$ be a projective hyper-Kähler manifold of dimension $2n$ with Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form $q_Y$, Fujiki constant $c_Y$ ($\int_Y\alpha^{2n}=c_Y\,q_Y(\alpha)^n$), ample class $h$, and $\Gamma\in CH^2(S\times Y)_{\mathbb Q}$. Then $\Gamma_*(T(S)_{\mathbb Q})\subseteq T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}:=NS(Y)_{\mathbb Q}^{\perp}$ and the adjoint of $\Gamma_*|_{T(S)}$ with respect to $(\,,)_S$ and $q_Y$ is induced by the algebraic correspondence $\Gamma^{\dagger}:=\frac{2n-1}{c_Y\,q_Y(h)^{n-1}}\;{}^t\Gamma\circ\Delta_*(h^{2n-2})$.

*Proof.* (a) is the projection formula. (b) $\Gamma_*$ is a morphism of Hodge structures and $T(S)$ is irreducible and not of type $(1,1)$, so its image lies in the transcendental part $T(Y)$ (an irreducible sub-Hodge structure of $H^2(Y,\mathbb Q)=NS_{\mathbb Q}\oplus T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ with $h^{2,0}\ne 0$ maps isomorphically to $T(Y)$ because $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(Y),NS_{\mathbb Q})=0$). Polarizing the Fujiki relation gives, for $x,y\in h^{\perp}$, $\int_Y x\cdot y\cdot h^{2n-2}=\frac{c_Y}{2n-1}q_Y(h)^{n-1}q_Y(x,y)$ (in the sum over the $(2n)!$ pairings only those pairing $x$ with $y$ survive, $2n\,(2n-2)!$ of them). Hence for $s\in T(S)$, $y\in T(Y)$: $q_Y(\Gamma_*s,y)=\frac{2n-1}{c_Yq_Y(h)^{n-1}}\int_Y\Gamma_*s\cdot(y\cdot h^{2n-2})=\frac{2n-1}{c_Yq_Y(h)^{n-1}}\big(s,({}^t\Gamma)_*(y\cdot h^{2n-2})\big)_S$. $\square$

## 2. Norms of Hodge morphisms and the field $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$

**Definition 2.1.** Let $Y$ be a projective K3 surface or a projective hyper-Kähler manifold, $T_Y:=T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}=NS(Y)_{\mathbb Q}^{\perp}$ with its form $q_Y$ (intersection form, resp. Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form); $T_Y$ is an irreducible Hodge structure with $h^{2,0}=1$, polarized by $q_Y$ (same proof as for K3 surfaces: a sub-Hodge structure not containing $H^{2,0}$ is of type $(1,1)$, hence contained in $T_Y\cap T_Y^{\perp}=0$). For a morphism of Hodge structures $\phi:T\to T_Y$ let $\phi^{\dagger}:T_Y\to T$ be its adjoint, $q_Y(\phi x,y)=(x,\phi^{\dagger}y)$; it is again a morphism of Hodge structures (both forms are morphisms of Hodge structures to $\mathbb Q(-2)$ and are non-degenerate). The **norm** of $\phi$ is $N(\phi):=\phi^{\dagger}\phi\in\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)=E$. We call $\phi$ a **Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$** if $q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=\lambda(x,y)$ for all $x,y$. We say $\phi$ is **algebraic** if $\phi=\Gamma_*|_T$ for an algebraic correspondence $\Gamma$.

**Lemma 2.2.** Assume RM. Let $\phi:T\to T_Y$ be a non-zero morphism of Hodge structures.
1. $\phi$ is an isomorphism, $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_Y)=\phi\cdot E$, and $N(\phi a)=a^{2}N(\phi)$ for $a\in E$.
2. $\phi$ is a Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda$ iff $N(\phi)=\lambda\in\mathbb Q$.
3. $N(\phi)$ is totally positive.
4. If $\phi$ is algebraic, so is $\phi^{\dagger}$, and $N(\phi)\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$.
5. $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is the subfield of $E$ generated by the norms $N(\phi)$ of all algebraic $\phi:T\to T_Y$ (all $Y$; it suffices to take $Y=S$).

*Proof.* (1) $T$ and $T_Y$ are irreducible, so a non-zero morphism is an isomorphism; then $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_Y)=\phi\circ\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T)$; and $N(\phi a)=a^{\dagger}\phi^{\dagger}\phi a=a\,N(\phi)\,a$ since $a^{\dagger}=a$ and $E$ is commutative. (2) $q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=(x,N(\phi)y)$, and $(x,ey)=\lambda(x,y)$ for all $x,y$ iff $e=\lambda$. (3) The form $(x,y)':=q_Y(\phi x,\phi y)=(x,N(\phi)y)$ is positive on $P$ (because $\phi$ maps $H^{2,0}$ to $H^{2,0}$, hence $P$ onto $P_Y$, where $q_Y$ is positive) and negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$ (mapped onto $T_Y{}^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$). On $T_\sigma$ one has $(x,N(\phi)y)=\sigma(N(\phi))(x,y)$ (Fact 1.2). For $\sigma\ne\sigma_1$, $T_\sigma\subset T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$ and both forms are negative definite there, so $\sigma(N(\phi))>0$. For $\sigma_1$ take $0\ne x\in P\subset T_{\sigma_1}$: $(x,x)>0$ and $(x,x)'>0$, so $\sigma_1(N(\phi))>0$. (4) Lemma 1.5: if $\phi=\Gamma_*|_T$ then $\phi^{\dagger}=(\Gamma^{\dagger})_*|_{T_Y}$ and $N(\phi)=(\Gamma^{\dagger}\circ\Gamma)_*|_T$. (5) Let $F$ be the subfield generated by the norms; $F\subseteq E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ by (4) and Lemma 1.3. Conversely, for $e\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ the maps $e,\,e+1:T\to T$ are algebraic with norms $e^{2},(e+1)^{2}$, so $2e=(e+1)^{2}-e^{2}-1\in F$. $\square$


**Remark 2.3 (the CM case, for contrast — known; proof corrected in round 2).** Let $E$ be CM with maximal real subfield $E_0$. Then $N(e)=\bar e e$ for $e\in E$. So every $u$ with $u\bar u=1$ is a Hodge isometry of $T$, since $(ux,uy)=(x,\bar u u y)=(x,y)$. By Fact 1.4 (with $S_1=S_2=S$) every such $u$ is algebraic.

*Claim: these $u$ span $E$ over $\mathbb Q$.* Write $E=E_0(\sqrt{-\delta})$ with $\delta\in E_0$ totally positive. Let $F$ be the $\mathbb Q$-span of the norm-one elements. It is closed under products and contains $1$, so it is a subfield of $E$. For $a\in E_0$ put $x=a+\sqrt{-\delta}$, so that $x\bar x=a^{2}+\delta\neq0$, and put $u=x/\bar x$. Then $u\bar u=1$ and
$$u+\bar u=\frac{x^{2}+\bar x^{2}}{x\bar x}=\frac{2(a^{2}-\delta)}{a^{2}+\delta}=2-\frac{4\delta}{a^{2}+\delta},\qquad u-\bar u=\frac{x^{2}-\bar x^{2}}{x\bar x}=\frac{4a\sqrt{-\delta}}{a^{2}+\delta}.$$
The steps are:

1. The first identity gives $\delta\,(a^{2}+\delta)^{-1}\in F$ for every $a\in E_0$. (Round 1 wrote "$(a^{2}+\delta)^{-1}\in F$" at this point. That does not follow until $\delta\in F$ is known; this was the slip the referees found.)
2. With $a=1$: $\delta/(1+\delta)\in F$. Since $F$ is a field, $(1+\delta)/\delta=1+\delta^{-1}\in F$, so $\delta\in F$.
3. Hence $(a^{2}+\delta)^{-1}=\delta^{-1}\cdot\delta(a^{2}+\delta)^{-1}\in F$. So $a^{2}+\delta\in F$, and therefore $a^{2}\in F$, for every $a\in E_0$.
4. Then $2a=(a+1)^{2}-a^{2}-1\in F$, so $E_0\subseteq F$.
5. Finally, $u-\bar u$ with $a=1$ gives $4\sqrt{-\delta}/(1+\delta)\in F$. Since $1+\delta\in E_0\subseteq F$, we get $\sqrt{-\delta}\in F$.

So $F=E$ and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$. $\square$

Thus **the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ whenever $S$ has CM**. This is due to Ramón-Marí (arXiv:math/0505357) for $\rho\ge5$ via Mukai–Nikulin, and holds in general by Buskin 2019 and Huybrechts 2019, Cor. 0.4(ii).

In the RM case the only Hodge isometries in $E$ are $\pm1$, because $e^{\dagger}e=e^{2}=1$. *As far as isometries are concerned*, this is the whole difference between the two cases.

## 3. Theorem A: the $H^2$-level similitude obstruction (scope corrected in round 2)

**Definition 3.1.** A *similitude chain* from $S$ to $S$ is a sequence $X_0=S,X_1,\dots,X_k=S$ of projective K3 surfaces and/or projective hyper-Kähler manifolds together with Hodge similitudes $\psi_i:T_{X_{i-1}}\to T_{X_i}$ ($i=1,\dots,k$; multipliers $\lambda_i\in\mathbb Q$, with respect to the intersection/BBF forms). Its *value* is $\psi_k\circ\dots\circ\psi_1\in E$. Let $\mathcal S(S)\subseteq E$ be the $\mathbb Q$-linear span of the values of all similitude chains. Let $E^{(2)}\subseteq E$ be the subfield generated by $\{e\in E:e^{2}\in\mathbb Q\}$; it is the compositum of the quadratic subfields of $E$, a multiquadratic field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{a_1},\dots,\sqrt{a_s})$ (Galois over $\mathbb Q$ with elementary abelian $2$-group).


**Lemma 3.1′ (subfields of multiquadratic fields).** Let $F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{a_1},\dots,\sqrt{a_s})$ with $a_i\in\mathbb Q^{*}$, and let $K\subseteq F$ be a subfield. Then $K$ is spanned over $\mathbb Q$ by $\{b\in K:\ b^{2}\in\mathbb Q\}$.

*Proof.* Put $F_0=\mathbb Q$ and $F_j=F_{j-1}(\sqrt{a_j})$, so $[F_j:F_{j-1}]\in\{1,2\}$. Discard the $a_j$ with $\sqrt{a_j}\in F_{j-1}$ and renumber. Then $F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{a_1},\dots,\sqrt{a_t})$ with $[F:\mathbb Q]=2^{t}$.

The $2^{t}$ monomials $\sqrt{a_I}:=\prod_{i\in I}\sqrt{a_i}$, for $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,t\}$, span the $\mathbb Q$-algebra $F$ (because $(\sqrt{a_i})^{2}\in\mathbb Q$). There are $2^t=[F:\mathbb Q]$ of them, so they form a basis.

$F/\mathbb Q$ is Galois. The map $g\mapsto(g(\sqrt{a_i})/\sqrt{a_i})_i$ is an injective homomorphism $G:=\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)\to\{\pm1\}^{t}$, and since $|G|=2^{t}$ it is bijective. Each $\sqrt{a_I}$ is a $G$-eigenvector with character $\chi_I(g)=\prod_{i\in I}g(\sqrt{a_i})/\sqrt{a_i}$, and the $\chi_I$ are pairwise distinct.

By the Galois correspondence, $K=F^{H}$ with $H=\operatorname{Gal}(F/K)$. An element $x=\sum_Ic_I\sqrt{a_I}$ is $H$-invariant iff $c_I=0$ whenever $\chi_I|_H\neq1$. So $K$ is spanned by the $\sqrt{a_I}$ with $\chi_I|_H=1$, and $(\sqrt{a_I})^{2}=\prod_{i\in I}a_i\in\mathbb Q$. $\square$

**Theorem A (sharpened in round 2).** Let $S$ be a projective K3 surface with real multiplication by $E$.
1. $\mathcal S(S)=E^{(2)}$.
2. Let $\mathcal S^{\mathrm{alg}}(S)\subseteq E$ be the $\mathbb Q$-span of the values of those similitude chains in which every $\psi_i$ is *algebraic*, i.e. induced by an algebraic correspondence. Then $\mathcal S^{\mathrm{alg}}(S)=E^{\mathrm{alg}}\cap E^{(2)}$.
3. Consequences:
   - (a) If $[E:\mathbb Q]$ is odd, then $\mathcal S(S)=\mathbb Q$.
   - (b) If $E$ is not multiquadratic, then $\mathcal S(S)\subsetneq E$. Examples: any field of degree $3$, $5$ or $7$, any quartic field that is not biquadratic, a cyclic sextic field.
   - (c) Hence, if $E$ is not multiquadratic and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$, then some algebraic element of $E$ is not a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of values of similitude chains.

*Proof.* (1) "$\subseteq$". Let $v=\psi_k\circ\dots\circ\psi_1$ be the value of a chain. Since $\psi_i^{\dagger}\psi_i=\lambda_i\cdot\mathrm{id}$, induction gives
$$v^{\dagger}v=\psi_1^{\dagger}\cdots\psi_k^{\dagger}\psi_k\cdots\psi_1=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_k\in\mathbb Q.$$
But $v\in E$ and $v^{\dagger}=v$ (RM), so $v^{2}\in\mathbb Q$ and $v\in E^{(2)}$.

(1) "$\supseteq$". Let $b\in E\setminus\{0\}$ with $b^{2}\in\mathbb Q$. Then $N(b)=b^{\dagger}b=b^{2}\in\mathbb Q$, so $b:T\to T$ is a Hodge similitude (Lemma 2.2(2)), i.e. the value of a chain of length one with $X_0=X_1=S$. So $\mathcal S(S)$ contains the generators of $E^{(2)}$. It remains to see that $\mathcal S(S)$ is a field:
- the value of the concatenation of two chains is the product of their values, so $\mathcal S(S)$ is closed under multiplication;
- it contains $1$ (the chain of length $0$) and is a $\mathbb Q$-subspace;
- a finite-dimensional $\mathbb Q$-subalgebra of a field is a subfield.

Hence $E^{(2)}\subseteq\mathcal S(S)$.

(2) "$\subseteq$". Compose the algebraic correspondences inducing $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_k$; the components in the appropriate codimension give an algebraic class in $CH^2(S\times S)_{\mathbb Q}$. Composites of correspondences act by the composite maps (Fulton, Ch. 16). Each correspondence maps $T_{X_{i-1}}$ into $T_{X_i}$ as $\psi_i$. So this class induces $v$ on $T$, hence $v\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ (Lemma 1.3); and $v\in E^{(2)}$ by (1).

(2) "$\supseteq$". $K:=E^{\mathrm{alg}}\cap E^{(2)}$ is a subfield of the multiquadratic field $E^{(2)}$. By Lemma 3.1′ it is spanned by elements $b$ with $b^{2}\in\mathbb Q$. Each such $b\neq0$ lies in $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ and is a Hodge similitude $T\to T$, i.e. an algebraic chain of length one.

(3) (a) A field of odd degree has no quadratic subfield, so $E^{(2)}=\mathbb Q$. (b) $E^{(2)}$ is multiquadratic, so $E^{(2)}=E$ forces $E$ to be multiquadratic. (c) Pick any $e\in E\setminus E^{(2)}$. $\square$

*Status.* Correct but elementary. The key identity $v^2=v^{\dagger}v\in\mathbb Q$ is Varesco 2023, Rem. 2.2: a Rosati-fixed Hodge similarity $\psi$ of multiplier $d$ satisfies $\psi^2=d$. The similitude computation for cycles through surfaces is van Geemen 2008, §7.9 (see Remark 3.7). Only the two equalities, as stated, may be new in this form.

**Lemma 3.2 (inventory of the $H^2$-level constructions; title corrected in round 2).** Each of the following maps is a Hodge similitude between transcendental lattices, with respect to the intersection and BBF forms, with the indicated multiplier. The lemma concerns only the action on transcendental lattices in degree $2$. It says nothing about other Künneth components of the same cycles, or about cup products with further classes (Remark 3.6).
1. Rational Hodge isometries $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ between projective K3 surfaces (Fact 1.4; "isogenies" in Huybrechts' sense), and rational Hodge isometries between $H^2$'s of projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of $K3^{[n]}$-type (Markman 2024): multiplier $1$.
2. The map induced on transcendental lattices by a twisted derived equivalence $D^{b}(S_1,\alpha_1)\cong D^{b}(S_2,\alpha_2)$ (Huybrechts–Stellari, Math. Ann. 332 (2005)): the equivalence induces a Hodge isometry of the twisted Mukai lattices $\widetilde H(S_i,B_i,\mathbb Z)$, which as lattices are the Mukai lattices $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Z)$ with the Hodge structure transported by the rational isometry $\exp(B_i)$ of $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Q)$; the transcendental part of $\widetilde H(S_i,\mathbb Q)$ is $T(S_i)_{\mathbb Q}$. So the induced rational map $T(S_1)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(S_2)_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Hodge isometry: multiplier $1$. (This is exactly why twisted derived equivalence implies isogeny, Huybrechts 2019.)
3. The map $T(S)\to T(M)$ induced by a (twisted) universal family on a smooth projective moduli space $M=M_H(v)$ of stable (twisted) sheaves, $v$ primitive, $\dim M=2n\ge 2$: Mukai's Hodge isometry $v^{\perp}\cong H^2(M,\mathbb Z)$ (for $n\ge2$; $v^{\perp}/\mathbb Zv$ for $n=1$) restricts to an isometry $T(S)\cong T(M)$ (Mukai 1987; O'Grady 1997; Yoshioka 2001; twisted case Yoshioka 2006): multiplier $1$.
4. Pull-back along a dominant rational map $f:X\dashrightarrow Y$ of degree $\delta$ between projective K3 surfaces: multiplier $\delta$; between projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of dimension $2n$ with $\operatorname{rk}T(Y)\ge 3$: multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ with $\lambda^{n}=\delta\,c_Y/c_X$ (Lemma 3.3).
5. The adjoint (transpose) of a similitude of multiplier $\lambda$: multiplier $\lambda$ (if $\psi^{\dagger}\psi=\lambda$ and $\psi$ is invertible then $\psi\psi^{\dagger}=\psi(\psi^{\dagger}\psi)\psi^{-1}=\lambda$).

**Lemma 3.3 (dominant rational maps).** Let $f:X\dashrightarrow Y$ be a dominant rational map of degree $\delta$ between projective K3 surfaces, or between projective hyper-Kähler manifolds of the same dimension $2n$ with $\operatorname{rk}T(Y)\ge3$. Then there is an algebraic morphism of Hodge structures $f^{*}:T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\to T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ (induced by the transpose of the closure of the graph of $f$) which is a Hodge similitude with multiplier $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$, $\lambda=\delta$ for K3 surfaces and $\lambda^{n}=\delta c_Y/c_X$ in general.

*Proof.* Let $\pi:\tilde X\to X$ be a resolution of the indeterminacy of $f$ by blow-ups along smooth centres and $g=f\circ\pi:\tilde X\to Y$, a generically finite surjective morphism of degree $\delta$. Then $H^2(\tilde X,\mathbb Q)=\pi^{*}H^2(X,\mathbb Q)\oplus A$, where $A$ is spanned by classes of exceptional divisors (algebraic), $\pi_*\pi^{*}=\mathrm{id}$ and $\pi_*A=0$ (an exceptional divisor maps onto a subvariety of codimension $\ge 2$). The transcendental part of $H^2(\tilde X)$ is therefore $\pi^{*}T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$. $g^{*}$ is a morphism of Hodge structures, injective because $g_*g^{*}=\delta$; being a morphism out of the irreducible, non-$(1,1)$ Hodge structure $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, it maps $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ into the transcendental part, i.e. $g^{*}T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}\subseteq\pi^{*}T(X)_{\mathbb Q}$. Put $f^{*}:=(\pi^{*})^{-1}g^{*}|_{T(Y)}$. With $\overline{\Gamma}_f:=(\pi,g)_*[\tilde X]\subset X\times Y$ one has $({}^t\overline{\Gamma}_f)_*=\pi_*g^{*}$, which agrees with $f^{*}$ on $T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$ (as $g^{*}x=\pi^{*}f^{*}x$ there): $f^{*}$ is algebraic. Multiplier: for $\alpha\in T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, $\int_X(f^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\int_{\tilde X}(\pi^{*}f^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\int_{\tilde X}(g^{*}\alpha)^{2n}=\delta\int_Y\alpha^{2n}$ (projection formula, $g_*g^{*}=\delta$ on $H^{4n}$). For K3 surfaces ($n=1$) this says $(f^{*}\alpha,f^{*}\alpha)=\delta(\alpha,\alpha)$, and polarization gives multiplier $\delta$. In general the Fujiki relations give $c_X\,q_X(f^{*}\alpha)^{n}=\delta c_Y\,q_Y(\alpha)^{n}$ for all $\alpha\in T(Y)_{\mathbb Q}$, hence as polynomial functions on $T(Y)_{\mathbb C}$. Since $q_Y|_{T(Y)}$ is a non-degenerate quadratic form in $\ge 3$ variables it is an irreducible polynomial; by unique factorisation in $\mathbb C[T(Y)]$, $q_Y$ divides $q_X\circ f^{*}$, so $q_X\circ f^{*}=\lambda\,q_Y$ with $\lambda^{n}=\delta c_Y/c_X$; $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$ because both forms are defined over $\mathbb Q$ and $q_Y\ne0$, and $\lambda>0$ because both forms are positive on the planes $P$, with $f^{*}P_Y=P_X$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4 (what $H^2$-level constructions cannot do; restricted in round 2).** Let $S$ have RM by a field $E$ that is not multiquadratic.

1. No element of $E\setminus E^{(2)}$ is a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites $T=T_{X_0}\to T_{X_1}\to\dots\to T_{X_k}=T$ of maps of the five kinds in Lemma 3.2. Equivalently, no such element lies in the $\mathbb Q$-linear category generated by these maps between transcendental lattices. For $[E:\mathbb Q]$ odd, this applies to every element of $E\setminus\mathbb Q$.
2. Ladder step (a) asked to decompose $e\in E$ as a sum of products of rational Hodge isometries between $S$ and its Fourier–Mukai partners. In this literal form it is impossible for every $E\ne\mathbb Q$, because products of isometries from $T$ to $T$ are $\pm1$.

*Proof.* Composition is bilinear, so every element of the category generated by the maps is a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites. A composite from $T$ to $T$ is the value of a similitude chain (Lemma 3.2), so Theorem A(1) applies. For the last sentence: a Hodge isometry $T\to T$ is an element $e\in E$ with $e^{2}=1$. $\square$

*Scope (round 2).* The round-1 gloss is **withdrawn**. It read: "*In other words, no combination of Buskin's theorem, Huybrechts' isogenies, twisted Fourier–Mukai partners, Mukai's moduli-space correspondences, Markman's theorem, finite covers and quotients, and their transposes can prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$*". The same applies to the round-1 verdict "this strategy can never prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ unless $E$ is multiquadratic".

Corollary 3.4 concerns only the action of the constructions between transcendental lattices in degree $2$. A correspondence built from the same cycles can act on $T$ through $H^{2k}(X)$, $k\ge2$, of a hyper-Kähler manifold $X$, or through cup products with further algebraic classes. Corollary 3.4 does not control such correspondences, and Remark 3.6(c) shows that this limitation is real. See G6.

**Remark 3.5 (non-vacuity).** Bayer-Fluckiger–van Geemen–Schütt (arXiv:2401.04072, 2024, Thm. A) prove: for every totally real field $E$ of degree $d$ and every $m\ge 3$ with $md\le 21$ there is an $(m-2)$-dimensional family of complex projective K3 surfaces with RM by $E$. Thus every totally real field of degree $2,\dots,7$ occurs; for $d\in\{3,5,7\}$ and for non-multiquadratic $E$ of degree $4$ or $6$ Theorem A applies with $E^{(2)}\subsetneq E$. Elsenhans–Jahnel (LMS J. Comput. Math. 2014; Michigan Math. J. 2023) give explicit families over $\mathbb Q$ with $\rho=16$ and RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ and by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$.

**Remark 3.6 (higher cohomology of hyper-Kähler fourfolds; conclusion corrected in round 2).** Let $X$ be a projective hyper-Kähler manifold of $K3^{[2]}$-type with $T(X)\cong T$ as Hodge structures. Normalise $q=q_X$ so that $\int_X\alpha^{4}=3q(\alpha)^{2}$. Two symmetric $4$-linear forms with the same diagonal coincide, so polarising gives, for all $a,b,c,d\in H^2(X,\mathbb Q)$,
$$\textstyle\int_X abcd=q(a,b)q(c,d)+q(a,c)q(b,d)+q(a,d)q(b,c).\tag{3.6.1}$$
Recall also that $H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q)=\operatorname{Sym}^{2}H^{2}(X,\mathbb Q)$ (Verbitsky; $b_4=276=\binom{24}{2}$).

*(a) Representation theory (unchanged from round 1; the referees found it correct).*
$$\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(-1),H^{4}(X))=\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T(-1),NS(X)\otimes T(X)).$$
So every Hodge morphism $T\to H^4(X)(1)$ has the form $x\mapsto\sum_jD_j\cdot\phi_j(x)$, where $(D_j)$ is a basis of $NS(X)_{\mathbb Q}$ and $\phi_j\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T(X))$.
*Proof.* Hodge morphisms are the morphisms equivariant for the Mumford–Tate group, which in the RM case is $\operatorname{Res}_{E/\mathbb Q}SO(T,\psi)$ (Zarhin); over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, $T=\bigoplus_\sigma T_\sigma$ with $T_\sigma$ the standard representation of the $\sigma$-th factor $SO_m$, and $\operatorname{Sym}^2T=\bigoplus_\sigma(\mathbf 1\oplus\operatorname{Sym}^2_0T_\sigma)\oplus\bigoplus_{\sigma<\tau}T_\sigma\otimes T_\tau$, whose irreducible constituents have dimensions $1$, $\frac{m(m+1)}2-1>m$, $m^{2}>m$ for $m\ge3$, so none is isomorphic to a $T_\rho$; the summands $\operatorname{Sym}^2NS$ are of type $(2,2)$. $\square$

*(b) The components are algebraic when the map is (new in round 2).* Let $\theta:T\to T(X)$ be an algebraic Hodge isometry, induced by $\Gamma_\theta\in CH^2(S\times X)_{\mathbb Q}$. For example, $X$ can be a moduli space of stable sheaves on a K3 surface isogenous to $S$ (Lemma 3.2(3) and Fact 1.4). Let $\Gamma\in CH^3(S\times X)_{\mathbb Q}$ induce on $T$ a map $x\mapsto\sum_jD_j\cdot\phi_j(x)$ as in (a). Let $(D_k^{\vee})$ be the $q$-dual basis of $NS(X)_{\mathbb Q}$; $q$ is non-degenerate there because $X$ is projective. **Claim:** for every $k$, $\theta^{\dagger}\phi_k\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$.

*Proof of the claim.* Consider the algebraic correspondence $\pi_T\circ{}^t\Gamma_\theta\circ\Delta_*(D_k^\vee)\circ\Gamma$. It acts in three steps:
- $\Gamma$ maps $H^2(S)\to H^4(X)$;
- $\Delta_*(D_k^\vee)$ is cup product with $D_k^\vee$, mapping $H^4(X)\to H^6(X)$;
- ${}^t\Gamma_\theta$ maps $H^6(X)\to H^2(S)$, with $\int_Ss'\cdot{}^t\Gamma_{\theta*}w=\int_X\Gamma_{\theta*}(s')\cdot w$.

For $s,s'\in T$, apply (3.6.1). The two terms containing $q(\theta s',D_k^\vee)$ or $q(\theta s',D_j)$ vanish because $T(X)\perp NS(X)$, so
$$\textstyle\big(s',{}^t\Gamma_{\theta*}(D_k^\vee\cdot\Gamma_*s)\big)_S=\int_X\theta(s')\,D_k^\vee\sum_jD_j\,\phi_j(s)=\sum_jq(D_k^\vee,D_j)\,q(\theta s',\phi_js)=(s',\theta^{\dagger}\phi_k s).$$
So the correspondence induces $\theta^{\dagger}\phi_k$ on $T$. $\square$

Conversely, $x\mapsto D_j\cdot\theta(ex)$ is algebraic for $e\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. So the algebraic Hodge morphisms $T\to H^4(X)(1)$ are exactly the maps $x\mapsto\sum_jD_j\cdot\theta(e_jx)$ with $e_j\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. Routing through $H^4(X)$ therefore neither adds anything to $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ nor excludes anything. In particular it gives no information on whether the $\phi_j$ are similitudes.

*(c) Why the round-1 conclusion was wrong: a non-similitude route through $H^6$ (new in round 2).* Round 1 concluded that "passing through $H^4$ of a moduli space does not help". Its reason was that the $\phi_j$ are similitudes "if produced by the constructions of Lemma 3.2". That premise is exactly the unproved point. For any $e\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ the map $x\mapsto h\cdot\theta(ex)$ has the allowed shape (referee 3, Test 2), and nothing forces $\phi_j$ to be a similitude.

More decisively, cup product with an algebraic class of degree $4$ leaves the world of similitudes. For $f\in\operatorname{End}_{HS}(T(X))$, choose a basis $(t_i)$ of $T(X)$ with $q$-dual basis $(t_i^\vee)$, and put
$$Q_f:=\sum_it_i\cdot f(t_i^\vee)\in H^4(X,\mathbb Q).$$
This is a Hodge class: it is the image of $f$ under the Hodge isomorphism $\operatorname{End}(T(X))\cong T(X)\otimes T(X)(2)$ given by $q$, followed by cup product. We use two identities: $\sum_iq(t_i,ft_i^\vee)=\operatorname{tr}(f|_{T(X)})$, and $\sum_iq(x,t_i)t_i^\vee=x$ for $x\in T(X)$. By (3.6.1), for $x,y\in T(X)$ and $f$ self-adjoint,
$$\textstyle\int_X x\cdot y\cdot Q_f=\sum_i\big[q(x,y)q(t_i,ft_i^\vee)+q(x,t_i)q(y,ft_i^\vee)+q(x,ft_i^\vee)q(y,t_i)\big]=q\big(x,(\operatorname{tr}(f)+2f)\,y\big).$$
Now take $\theta,\Gamma_\theta$ as in (b), $e\in E$ and $f:=\theta e\theta^{-1}$. The correspondence $\pi_T\circ{}^t\Gamma_\theta\circ\Delta_*(Q_f)\circ\Gamma_\theta$ runs $H^2(S)\to H^2(X)\xrightarrow{\cdot Q_f}H^6(X)\to H^2(S)$. By the same adjunction it induces $\operatorname{tr}(f)+2e$ on $T$.

Hence **if the Hodge class $Q_f\in H^4(X)$ is algebraic, then $\operatorname{tr}(f)+2e$, and therefore $e$, lies in $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$, for every $e\in E$ including $e\notin E^{(2)}$.** The route consists of an algebraic isometry, cup product with a degree-$4$ class, and the transpose of an algebraic isometry. Theorem A does not apply to the middle step.

Whether $Q_f$ is algebraic is itself an open instance of the Hodge conjecture on $X$. For $X=S^{[2]}$ it is equivalent to the algebraicity of $\gamma_e$. Indeed, let $\rho:\mathrm{Bl}_\Delta(S\times S)\to S^{[2]}$ be the double cover and $\sigma$ the blow-down; then $\rho^{*}Q_f=\sigma^{*}\big(2\gamma_e+\operatorname{tr}(e)(p_1^*[\mathrm{pt}]+p_2^*[\mathrm{pt}])\big)$, $\rho_*\rho^*=2$ and $\sigma_*\sigma^*=1$. So this is not a new *source* of cycles. But no argument in this file excludes it. Theorem A is therefore an obstruction to $H^2$-level similitude chains and to nothing more. Referee 2 noted the same phenomenon for $K3^{[n]}$, $n\ge3$, where $T\subset T\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^2T\subset\operatorname{Sym}^3H^2$ via $x\mapsto x\cdot Q_f$.

**Remark 3.7 (where a non-similitude must come from; van Geemen 2008, §7.9, with complete proof).** Every class in $CH^2(S\times S)_{\mathbb Q}$ is $\sum_in_i[Z_i]$ with $Z_i\subset S\times S$ irreducible surfaces. Let $\tilde Z_i\to Z_i$ be resolutions and $g_{i,1},g_{i,2}:\tilde Z_i\to S$ the induced projections. By the projection formula, $[Z_i]_*=g_{i,2*}g_{i,1}^{*}$.

*Non-dominant components contribute nothing on $T$.*
- If $g_{i,1}$ is not dominant, $g_{i,1}^{*}|_T$ factors through $H^2$ of a curve or a point. That group is of type $(1,1)$, so the map vanishes on $T$.
- If $g_{i,2}$ is not dominant, $g_{i,2*}$ has image in the span of classes of curves. A Hodge morphism $T\to NS_{\mathbb Q}$ is $0$.

*Dominant components.* Now let $g_1,g_2:\tilde Z\to S$ be dominant, hence generically finite, of degrees $d_1,d_2$, and suppose $g_1^*T=g_2^*T$ in $H^2(\tilde Z,\mathbb Q)$. This holds automatically if $p_g(\tilde Z)=1$. Indeed, $g_i^*\omega_S\neq0$ because $g_i$ is étale somewhere, so both copies are irreducible sub-Hodge structures containing the line $H^{2,0}(\tilde Z)$. They therefore meet non-trivially, and so they are equal.

For $x\in T$ write $g_1^*x=g_2^*y$ and put $f:=g_{2*}g_1^*$. Then $f(x)=d_2y$ and
$$d_2(y,y')=\textstyle\int g_2^*y\,g_2^*y'=\int g_1^*x\,g_1^*x'=d_1(x,x').$$
So $(fx,fx')=d_1d_2(x,x')$: $f$ is a similitude, and $f\in E^{(2)}$.

**Consequently, if some $e\in E\setminus E^{(2)}$ is algebraic, every cycle representing $\gamma_e$ has a component $\tilde Z$ with $p_g(\tilde Z)\ge2$ on which the two pulled-back copies of $T$ differ.** This is a description, not an obstruction: it says where the content of the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ must lie, and nothing excludes such components.

## 4. The real quadratic case: an exact reduction, and what is actually known

Let $\Lambda(S)\subseteq\mathbb Q_{>0}/\mathbb Q^{*2}$ be the set of classes of multipliers $\lambda$ for which there exist a projective K3 surface $Y$ and an **algebraic** Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier $\lambda$. Clearly $1\in\Lambda(S)$ (the identity) and $\Lambda(S)$ depends only on the isogeny class of $S$ (compose with Buskin's isometries).

**Proposition 4.1 (the quadratic part of $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$.
1. If $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$, then *every* Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda$ from $T$ to the transcendental lattice of *any* projective K3 surface is algebraic.
2. For $a\in E$ with $a^{2}=\lambda\in\mathbb Q$: $\;a\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}\iff\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$.
3. Consequently $E^{\mathrm{alg}}\supseteq E^{(2)}$ if and only if $\Lambda(S)\supseteq\{\lambda:\sqrt\lambda\in E\}$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $\psi:T\to T_Y$ be algebraic with $N(\psi)=\lambda$ and $\phi:T\to T_Z$ any Hodge similitude with $N(\phi)=\lambda$. Then $\phi\psi^{-1}=\lambda^{-1}\phi\psi^{\dagger}:T_Y\to T_Z$ satisfies $N(\phi\psi^{-1})=\lambda^{-2}\,\psi\,\phi^{\dagger}\phi\,\psi^{\dagger}=\lambda^{-2}\cdot\lambda\cdot\psi\psi^{\dagger}=1$ (Lemma 3.2(5)), so it is a rational Hodge isometry between transcendental lattices of projective K3 surfaces, algebraic by Fact 1.4; hence $\phi=(\phi\psi^{-1})\circ\psi$ is algebraic. (2) "$\Rightarrow$": $a$ is itself an algebraic Hodge similitude $T\to T$ of multiplier $a^{2}=\lambda$ (Lemma 2.2(2)). "$\Leftarrow$": let $\psi:T\to T_Y$ be algebraic with $N(\psi)=\lambda$. Then $\theta:=\psi\circ a^{-1}:T\to T_Y$ is a morphism of Hodge structures with $N(\theta)=a^{-2}\lambda=1$, i.e. a rational Hodge isometry, algebraic by Fact 1.4; so $a=\theta^{-1}\psi=\theta^{\dagger}\psi$ is algebraic (Lemma 1.5). (3) $E^{(2)}$ is generated by the $a$ with $a^{2}\in\mathbb Q$, and $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a field (Lemma 1.3). $\square$

**Theorem C (real quadratic RM: exact reduction).** Let $S$ have RM by $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$, $D>1$ square-free. The following are equivalent:
1. the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$;
2. $\sqrt D\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$;
3. $D\in\Lambda(S)$: there exist a projective K3 surface $Y$ and an algebraic Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier $Dc^{2}$, $c\in\mathbb Q^{*}$;
4. for every projective K3 surface $Y$, every Hodge similitude $T\to T_Y$ of multiplier in $D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$ is algebraic;
5. the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times Y$ for one (equivalently, every) projective K3 surface $Y$ isogenous to $S$.

*Proof.* (1)$\Leftrightarrow$(2): $E=\mathbb Q\oplus\mathbb Q\sqrt D$, $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a field (Lemma 1.3). (2)$\Leftrightarrow$(3): Proposition 4.1(2) with $a=\sqrt D$. (3)$\Rightarrow$(4): Proposition 4.1(1). (4)$\Rightarrow$(3): apply (4) to $Y=S$ and the similitude $\sqrt D$. (1)$\Leftrightarrow$(5): for $Y$ isogenous to $S$ fix an algebraic Hodge isometry $\theta:T\to T_Y$; the Hodge classes on $S\times Y$ are $\mathbb Q^{2}\oplus NS_S\otimes NS_Y\oplus\{\text{classes inducing }\theta e,\ e\in E\}$ (same proof as Lemma 1.3), and $\theta e$ is algebraic iff $e=\theta^{\dagger}(\theta e)$ is. $\square$

**Theorem C′ (a sufficient condition).** Let $S$ have RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$. Suppose there are a projective K3 surface $S'$ isogenous to $S$ (for instance $S'=S$, or any surface connected to $S$ by a chain of twisted derived equivalences, Huybrechts 2019, Thm. 0.1), a projective K3 surface $S''$, and a dominant rational map $S'\dashrightarrow S''$ or $S''\dashrightarrow S'$ of degree $\delta\in D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$. Then the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$.

*Proof.* Lemma 3.3 gives an algebraic Hodge similitude of multiplier $\delta$ between $T_{S'}$ and $T_{S''}$, in one direction; its adjoint is algebraic (Lemma 1.5) with the same multiplier (Lemma 3.2(5)), so there is an algebraic similitude $T_{S'}\to T_{S''}$ of multiplier $\delta$. Composing with an algebraic Hodge isometry $T\to T_{S'}$ (Fact 1.4) gives $\delta\in\Lambda(S)$; apply Theorem C, (3)$\Rightarrow$(1). $\square$

*Status of Proposition 4.1 and Theorems C, C′ (round 2).* All three are correct (all three referees). They are known in substance: Proposition 4.1(1) and Theorem C′ are the argument of Varesco 2023, Thm. 2.1 (quotient map plus Buskin isometry). Theorem C repackages Varesco 2023, §§0.2 and 2. Theorem C′ only drops the Galois hypothesis, and I know no new instance of it (Corollary 4.2(b), (d)).

**Corollary 4.2 (Varesco's theorem, and the reach of finite maps; corrected in round 2).**

(a) Suppose a K3 surface Hodge isometric to $S$ carries a symplectic automorphism of order $p\in\{2,3\}$, and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt p)\subseteq E$. Then $\sqrt p\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. If moreover $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p)$, the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$.

This is Varesco's theorem (Math. Z. 2023, arXiv:2304.02519, Thm. 0.1 = Thms. 2.1, 2.9, 2.15). His proof is the mechanism of Theorem C′: the quotient map is an algebraic similitude of multiplier $p$, and composing it with a Buskin isometry recovers $\sqrt p$.

*Citation correction.* Round 1 cited "Thm. 2.1 / 5.3". Varesco's Theorem 5.3 is a different statement: if the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture holds for $S$ and $S'$, every Hodge similarity $T(S)\to T(S')$ is algebraic.

(b) Suppose a K3 surface $S'$ isogenous to $S$ carries a faithful symplectic action of a finite group $G$ with $|G|\in D\cdot\mathbb Q^{*2}$, and $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$. Then the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$. Indeed, the quotient map $S'\dashrightarrow\widetilde{S'/G}$ has degree $|G|$, $\widetilde{S'/G}$ is a K3 surface (Nikulin 1980), and Theorem C′ applies.

*Added in round 2: for $D=p\in\{2,3\}$ this adds nothing to (a).* Here $v_p(|G|)$ is odd, so $p\mid|G|$. By Cauchy's theorem $G$ contains an element of order $p$, and it acts symplectically. So (b) can be new only for $D=6$, and there existence is unverified (G3).

(c) In the RM range $\rho\le16$ only $|G|$ with prime factors $2,3$ occur: a symplectic automorphism of order $5$ (resp. $7$) has coinvariant lattice of rank $16$ (resp. $18$) (Nikulin 1980), forcing $\rho\ge17$ (resp. $\ge19$); hence (b) can only ever produce $D\in\{2,3,6\}$. The existence constraints are $\rho\ge 9$ for $\mathbb Z/2$ (coinvariant lattice $E_8(-2)$ of rank $8$; conversely a primitive embedding $E_8(-2)\hookrightarrow NS(S')$ gives a Nikulin involution, van Geemen–Sarti 2007), $\rho\ge13$ for $\mathbb Z/3$ (rank $12$), and $\rho\ge15$ for $S_3$ (rank $14$: $\operatorname{rk}H^2(S)^{S_3}=\frac16(22+3\cdot6+2\cdot4)=8$ by the Lefschetz fixed point formula, a symplectic involution having $8$ fixed points and an element of order $3$ having $6$); with $d=2$ the Picard number is even, so $\rho\in\{10,12,14,16\}$, $\{14,16\}$, $\{16\}$ respectively.

(d) Theorem C′ does not require the map to be Galois. However, I know of no non-Galois dominant rational map of non-square degree between K3 surfaces in the RM range that would give a case not already covered by (a) or by §4.4.

*Correction (round 2).* Round 1 called Elsenhans–Jahnel's explicit $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ family "a precise, concrete open sub-problem". That is **false**:
- By Bayer-Fluckiger–van Geemen–Schütt (arXiv:2401.04072, Ex. 11.2), the explicit Elsenhans–Jahnel examples with $d=2$ (2014) and with $d=2,5$ (2023) are double covers of $\mathbb P^2$ branched along six lines.
- For every such surface the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ is Schlickewei's theorem (J. Algebra 324 (2010), Thm. 2).

In particular $\sqrt5$ is algebraic on those surfaces. As far as I know, this algebraic similitude of non-square multiplier does not come from any finite map.

**Remark 4.3 (the problem lives inside the isogeny class; corrected in round 2).** Let $S$ have RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$. A K3 surface $Y$ is Hodge-similar to $S$ with multiplier $D$ iff it is isogenous to $S$. Indeed, if $N(\phi)=D$ then $N(\phi\circ\sqrt D^{-1})=D^{-1}\cdot D=1$; conversely, $N(\theta\sqrt D)=D$ for an isometry $\theta$.

So all the surfaces $S''$ in Theorem C′ are automatically isogenous to $S$. By Huybrechts 2019 (Thm. 0.1, Cor. 1.4), such surfaces are connected to $S$ by a *chain* of twisted derived equivalences. In general they are not single twisted Fourier–Mukai partners of $S$ (loc. cit., Rem. 1.3). The real quadratic problem is therefore a question about the isogeny class of $S$: *is the self-similitude $\sqrt D$ of $T$ algebraic?*

Buskin's theorem says that the isometries are algebraic. Algebraic similitudes of non-square multiplier are known from two sources:
- (i) finite maps (Varesco 2023, Thm. 2.1; Theorem C′);
- (ii) algebraic Kuga–Satake correspondences. By Varesco 2023, Thm. 5.3 (deduced there from Cor. 4.6), the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture for $S$ and $S'$ makes every Hodge similarity $T(S)\to T(S')$ algebraic. This covers the families in §4.4.

*Correction (round 2).* Round 1 said that "nothing known says anything about the similitudes of non-square multiplier, except when a finite map supplies one". That was false.

**4.4 Known cases of the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ with RM (literature; checked against the sources in round 2, not re-proved).**

1. *Six-line double planes.* Let $S$ be any K3 surface that is a double cover of $\mathbb P^2$ branched along six lines. Then the Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ (Schlickewei 2010, Thm. 2, via Paranjape's algebraic Kuga–Satake correspondence). Varesco 2022 (below) gives it for all powers.
   - RM occurs in one-parameter subfamilies ($\rho=16$, $m=3$).
   - RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)$ occurs exactly for $d$ a sum of two squares (BvGS 2024, Ex. 11.2). Existence was shown for odd $d$ by van Geemen 2008, Ex. 3.4, and for all such $d$ by Elsenhans–Jahnel 2014.
2. *Families of generic Picard number $16$ with algebraic Kuga–Satake correspondence.*
   - Varesco (arXiv:2203.09778, Thm. 0.2) proves the Hodge conjecture for all powers of every member of a four-dimensional family with $T_{\mathbb Q}\cong U_{\mathbb Q}^2\oplus\langle a\rangle\oplus\langle b\rangle$, provided the Kuga–Satake correspondence is algebraic on it. He applies this to the six-line family and to desingularised $15$-nodal quartics in $\mathbb P^4$, whose Kuga–Satake correspondence is algebraic by Ingalls–Logan–Patashnick.
   - Floccari (Compositio 160 (2024), arXiv:2210.02948) proves the Kuga–Satake Hodge conjecture for countably many four-dimensional families with $T\cong T(K)(2)$, $K$ a generalized Kummer sixfold. Combined with Varesco 2023, Thm. 5.3, this gives the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ on their one-dimensional real quadratic RM subfamilies (Varesco 2023, end of §5).
3. *Symplectic automorphisms* (Corollary 4.2(a); Varesco 2023, Thms. 2.9, 2.15, Props. 2.6, 2.12).
   - $\sqrt2$ is algebraic for every K3 surface with $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\subseteq E$ that is Hodge isometric to one with a Nikulin involution. These form families of dimension $\le4$ ($\rho\ge10$), and the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ follows when $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$.
   - Likewise for $\sqrt3$ and order-$3$ automorphisms, in families of dimension $\le2$ ($\rho\ge14$).

In every known case $E$ is real quadratic; in cases 1–2, $\rho=16$. Combining this list with Corollary 4.2(c), the following are, in particular, *not covered by any result I could find*:
- RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ with $D\not\equiv2,3\pmod{\mathbb Q^{*2}}$ and $\rho\le14$;
- RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ with $\rho\le8$;
- RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$ with $\rho\le12$;
- every RM field of degree $\ge3$.

Varesco 2023, Rem. 2.10, says explicitly that the quartic case with $\dim T=12$ is not reached. This list is "to my knowledge", not a theorem.

## 5. Twists $S_q$, and the shape of the general problem

**Proposition 5.1 (existence of twists).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$ with $\rho(S)\ge3$ (i.e. $r=\operatorname{rk}T\le19$), and let $q\in E$ be totally positive. Then there exist a projective K3 surface $S_q$ and an isomorphism of Hodge structures $\phi_q:T\to T_{S_q}$ with $N(\phi_q)=q$, i.e. $(\phi_qx,\phi_qy)_{S_q}=(x,qy)$ for all $x,y\in T$. Any two such surfaces are isogenous; $S_q$ is isogenous to $S$ iff $q\in E^{*2}$, and Hodge-similar to $S$ iff $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\cdot E^{*2}$.

*Proof.* (i) The form $(x,y)_q:=(x,qy)$ on $T$ is symmetric ($q^{\dagger}=q$), non-degenerate, and on $T_\sigma$ equals $\sigma(q)(\,,)$ with $\sigma(q)>0$; hence it has signature $(2,r-2)$, is positive on $P$, negative definite on $T^{1,1}_{\mathbb R}$, and for $\omega$ spanning $H^{2,0}$ one has $(\omega,\omega)_q=\sigma_1(q)(\omega,\omega)=0$ and $(\omega,\bar\omega)_q=\sigma_1(q)(\omega,\bar\omega)>0$ (the $\mathbb C$-bilinear extension; $q\omega=\sigma_1(q)\omega$ by Fact 1.2).
(ii) The rational quadratic space $V=(T,(\,,)_q)$, of dimension $r\le19$ and signature $(2,r-2)$, embeds isometrically into $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}:=\Lambda_{K3}\otimes\mathbb Q$ (signature $(3,19)$). Indeed, by the classification of quadratic forms over $\mathbb Q$ (Serre, *A course in arithmetic*, Ch. IV, Prop. 6, Prop. 7, Thm. 9) a quadratic space $V'$ over $\mathbb Q$ of dimension $22-r\ge3$ exists with the local invariants $d(V'_v)=d(\Lambda)d(V)$, $\varepsilon_v(V')=\varepsilon_v(\Lambda)\varepsilon_v(V)\,(d(V),d(V'))_v$ for all places $v$ and signature $(1,21-r)$: these data are consistent at $\infty$ (they are the invariants of the real orthogonal complement), satisfy the product formula (because $\Lambda$, $V$ and the Hilbert symbol do), and in dimension $\ge3$ every pair $(d,\varepsilon_v)$ is realised locally. Then $V\oplus V'$ and $\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$ have the same dimension, discriminant, signature and Hasse invariants, hence are isometric (Hasse–Minkowski), which gives $\iota:V\hookrightarrow\Lambda_{\mathbb Q}$.
(iii) $L:=\iota(V)\cap\Lambda_{K3}$ is a primitive sublattice, even (as a sublattice of the even lattice $\Lambda_{K3}$), with $L_{\mathbb Q}=\iota(V)$. The vector $\omega':=\iota(\omega)\in L_{\mathbb C}$ satisfies $(\omega',\omega')=0$, $(\omega',\bar\omega')>0$. By the surjectivity of the period map for K3 surfaces (Todorov 1980, Looijenga 1981; Huybrechts, *Lectures on K3 surfaces*, Ch. 7, Thm. 4.1) there is a K3 surface $S_q$ with a marking $H^2(S_q,\mathbb Z)\cong\Lambda_{K3}$ under which $H^{2,0}(S_q)=\mathbb C\omega'$.
(iv) $NS(S_q)=\omega'^{\perp}\cap\Lambda_{K3}\supseteq L^{\perp}$, which has signature $(1,21-r)$; so $NS(S_q)$ contains a class $h$ with $h^{2}>0$, and $S_q$ is projective (Riemann–Roch gives $h^0(h)+h^0(-h)\ge2+h^{2}/2$, so $\pm h$ is effective and big, and a Kähler surface with a big line bundle is projective).
(v) $L_{\mathbb Q}$ is a sub-Hodge structure of $H^2(S_q,\mathbb Q)$: it contains $\omega'$ and $\bar\omega'$, on whose span the form is non-degenerate, so $L_{\mathbb C}=\langle\omega',\bar\omega'\rangle\oplus(L_{\mathbb C}\cap\langle\omega',\bar\omega'\rangle^{\perp})$ is the induced Hodge decomposition. Moreover $\iota:T\to L_{\mathbb Q}$ is an isomorphism of Hodge structures: both sides have $H^{2,0}$ spanned by $\omega$ resp. $\omega'$, and the $(1,1)$-part of $T$ is the orthogonal of $\{\omega,\bar\omega\}$ for $(\,,)$, which coincides with the orthogonal for $(\,,)_q$ because $q\omega=\sigma_1(q)\omega$. Hence $L_{\mathbb Q}$ is irreducible and contains $H^{2,0}(S_q)$; since $T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}$ is the smallest sub-Hodge structure containing $H^{2,0}(S_q)$ and is contained in $L_{\mathbb Q}$, $T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}=L_{\mathbb Q}$ and $\phi_q:=\iota$ is a Hodge isometry $(T,(\,,)_q)\to T(S_q)_{\mathbb Q}$, i.e. $N(\phi_q)=q$.
(vi) Uniqueness up to isogeny: if $\phi'_q:T\to T_{S'_q}$ is another such map, $\phi'_q\phi_q^{-1}$ is a Hodge isometry. By Lemma 2.2(1) every $\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is $\phi_qa$ with $N(\phi)=qa^{2}$; so an isometry (resp. similitude) exists iff $q\in E^{*2}$ (resp. $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}E^{*2}$). $\square$

*Attribution (round 2).* This is van Geemen's real twist (2008, §4.7). He states it for $\dim T\le11$, using Nikulin's embedding theorem. The extension to $\dim T\le19$ above via Hasse–Minkowski and the period map is routine; all referees found it correct. Van Geemen (§7.8) also poses the problem behind Proposition 5.2: is the identity $T_{S}\to T_{S_a}$ algebraic?

**Proposition 5.2 (algebraic maps to the twists; item (3) corrected in round 2).** Let $S$ have RM by $E$ with $\rho(S)\ge3$. Let $q\in E$ be totally positive, with $S_q,\phi_q$ as in Proposition 5.1.
1. If some $\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is algebraic, then $E^{\mathrm{alg}}\supseteq\mathbb Q(N(\phi))$ and $N(\phi)\in q\cdot E^{*2}$.
2. *(Tautological.)* The Hodge conjecture holds for $S\times S$ iff there are totally positive $q_1,\dots,q_k\in E$ and algebraic $\phi_i\in\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_{q_i}})$ with $\mathbb Q\big(N(\phi_1),\dots,N(\phi_k)\big)=E$.
3. Parts (3a)–(3c):
   - **(3a)** If $q\notin\mathbb Q_{>0}\cdot E^{*2}$, no element of $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is a Hodge similitude. Hence every $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites of maps of Lemma 3.2 from $T$ to $T_{S_q}$ is $0$.
   - **(3b)** Let $q=\lambda a^{2}$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$ and $a\in E^{*}$, and put $\psi:=\phi_q a^{-1}$, a Hodge similitude of multiplier $\lambda$. The similitudes in $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ are exactly the $\psi b$ with $b\in E^{*}$ and $b^{2}\in\mathbb Q$. Their multipliers $\lambda b^{2}$ fill the set $\lambda\cdot(\mathbb Q^{*}\cap E^{*2})$. So $q$ determines the multiplier class mod $\mathbb Q^{*2}$ only up to the finite group $(\mathbb Q^{*}\cap E^{*2})/\mathbb Q^{*2}$, which is non-trivial as soon as $E$ has a quadratic subfield. Such a $\psi b$ is algebraic iff $\lambda b^{2}\in\Lambda(S)$.
   - **(3c)** The set $A_q:=\{b\in E:\ \psi b\ \text{is algebraic}\}$ is either $\{0\}$ or $b_0E^{\mathrm{alg}}$ for some $b_0\in E^{*}$ with $b_0^{2}\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. In particular, $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ contains an algebraic similitude of multiplier in $\lambda\mathbb Q^{*2}$ iff $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$. In that case its algebraic elements are exactly $\psi E^{\mathrm{alg}}=\phi_qa^{-1}E^{\mathrm{alg}}$.
4. Suppose $E$ is not multiquadratic. Then the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ requires an algebraic element of $E$ that is not a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of composites of Hodge similitudes (Theorem A(3c)). When $E$ is multiquadratic, no such element exists, since $E=E^{(2)}=\mathcal S(S)$. For $E$ of degree $\ge3$, I know of no K3 surface with RM by $E$ for which the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ is known (§4.4).

*Proof.* (1) Lemma 2.2(4),(5) and Lemma 1.3. By Lemma 2.2(1), $N(\phi_qb)=qb^{2}$.

(2) "$\Leftarrow$": by Lemma 2.2(5) the norms of algebraic maps lie in the field $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. "$\Rightarrow$": take $a\in E$ with $\mathbb Q(a)=E$. Put $q_1=a^{2}$, $q_2=(a+1)^{2}$, and take $S_{q_1}=S_{q_2}=S$ with $\phi_{q_1}=a$, $\phi_{q_2}=a+1$; these are allowed by Lemma 2.2(1),(2). They are algebraic by hypothesis, and $2a=(a+1)^2-a^2-1\in\mathbb Q(a^{2},(a+1)^{2})$. This direction takes $S_q=S$, which is why the statement is tautological.

(3a) By Lemma 2.2(1), every element of $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ is $\phi_qb$ with norm $qb^{2}$. This lies in $\mathbb Q$ iff $q\in\mathbb Q^{*}E^{*2}$. By total positivity the rational factor is then positive, i.e. $q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}E^{*2}$. A composite of similitudes is a similitude with non-zero multiplier, so it cannot land in $T_{S_q}$. Composition is bilinear, so every combination of composites ending at $T_{S_q}$ is a combination of an empty set of maps, i.e. $0$.

(3b) Every element is $\psi b$ with $b\in E$, and $N(\psi b)=b^{2}N(\psi)=\lambda b^{2}$. So for $b\neq0$, $\psi b$ is a similitude iff $b^{2}\in\mathbb Q$. The set $\{b^{2}:b\in E^{*},b^{2}\in\mathbb Q\}$ equals $\mathbb Q^{*}\cap E^{*2}$. The map $c\mapsto\mathbb Q(\sqrt c)$ shows that $(\mathbb Q^{*}\cap E^{*2})/\mathbb Q^{*2}$ is finite, and non-trivial iff $E$ has a quadratic subfield.

For algebraicity: if $\psi b$ is algebraic, then $\lambda b^{2}\in\Lambda(S)$ by definition, since $S_q$ is a projective K3 surface. Conversely, if $\lambda b^{2}\in\Lambda(S)$, then $\psi b$ is algebraic by Proposition 4.1(1).

(3c) $A_q$ is closed under addition. It is also closed under multiplication by $E^{\mathrm{alg}}$, because $\psi be=(\psi b)\circ e$ and composites of algebraic maps are algebraic.

Let $b,b'\in A_q$ with $b\neq0$. By Lemma 1.5(a), $(\psi b)^{\dagger}$ is induced by the transpose correspondence. For $y\in T_{S_q}$ and $x\in NS(S)$ we have $(x,{}^t\Gamma_*y)=(\Gamma_*x,y)=0$, because $\Gamma_*x\in NS(S_q)$; so the transpose maps $T_{S_q}$ into $T$. Hence $(\psi b)^{\dagger}(\psi b')=b\,\psi^{\dagger}\psi\,b'=\lambda bb'$ is algebraic, and $bb'\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. In particular $b^{2}\in E^{\mathrm{alg}}$. Then
$$b'=(bb')\,(b^{2})^{-1}\,b\in bE^{\mathrm{alg}}.$$
So $A_q=bE^{\mathrm{alg}}$.

For the last sentence: $\psi b$ has multiplier in $\lambda\mathbb Q^{*2}$ iff $b^{2}\in\mathbb Q^{*2}$, iff $b\in\mathbb Q^{*}$. So an algebraic similitude of such a multiplier exists iff $\psi$ is algebraic, iff $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$ by (3b). Then $1\in A_q$ and $A_q=E^{\mathrm{alg}}$.

(4) If $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E\neq E^{(2)}$, pick $e\in E\setminus E^{(2)}$. It is algebraic, and it is not in $\mathcal S(S)$ by Theorem A(1). The last sentence of (4) is a literature statement (§4.4). $\square$

*The round-1 sentence and why it was false.* Round 1 asserted: "If $q=\lambda a^{2}$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$, then $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ contains an algebraic element iff $\lambda\in\Lambda(S)$, and then all of $\phi_qE^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is algebraic." Two things are wrong with it:
- By (3b), $\lambda$ is determined by $q$ only modulo $\mathbb Q^{*}\cap E^{*2}$.
- By (3c), $\phi_qE^{\mathrm{alg}}=\psi aE^{\mathrm{alg}}$ is algebraic iff $a\in A_q$.

*Counterexample (both referees).* Take $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ and $q=D=D\cdot1^{2}=1\cdot(\sqrt D)^{2}$. Then $q\in E^{*2}$, so $S_q$ is isogenous to $S$, and $\operatorname{Hom}_{HS}(T,T_{S_q})$ always contains the algebraic isometry $\phi_q\sqrt D^{-1}$ (Fact 1.4).
- Read with $(\lambda,a)=(D,1)$, the sentence gives $D\in\Lambda(S)$.
- Read with $(\lambda,a)=(1,\sqrt D)$, it makes $\phi_q$ algebraic. Then $\sqrt D=(\phi_q\sqrt D^{-1})^{\dagger}\phi_q$ is algebraic.

Either reading proves the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ for every real-quadratic RM surface with $\rho\ge3$, by Theorem C. That is open (§4.4). The round-1 citation (Proposition 4.1(1), Lemma 2.2(1)) proves only (3b)–(3c). No other result used the false sentence.

**Remark 5.3 (on the "exact reduction" requested in ladder step (c); restricted in round 2).**

(a) For $E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ the exact reduction is Theorem C: the RM classes are algebraic iff the similitudes of multiplier $D$ out of $T$ are algebraic. For non-multiquadratic $E$, Theorem A shows only this much: a statement whose output is the algebraicity of some *$H^2$-level similitudes* cannot imply $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$. Examples are statements about Brauer classes, twisted derived equivalences, or the action of moduli spaces of (twisted) sheaves on $H^2$. Round 1 claimed this for every statement about Brauer classes, twisted sheaves or moduli spaces; that claim is withdrawn. Statements that produce cycles of higher degree on hyper-Kähler manifolds are not covered (Remark 3.6(c)).

(b) *Kuga–Satake.* If the Kuga–Satake correspondence of $S$ is algebraic, then $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$ for real quadratic $E$. Indeed, Varesco 2023, Thm. 5.3 makes every Hodge similarity algebraic, and $1,\sqrt D$ are similitudes. Schlickewei 2010 (Thm. 1 and §4) computes $\operatorname{End}^0$ of the Kuga–Satake variety of an RM surface following van Geemen, and proves the six-line case. For $E$ of higher degree, $E$ is not spanned by similitudes. I have not checked whether an algebraic Kuga–Satake correspondence alone gives $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$ there, and I do not claim it. Nor do I claim a converse ("RM classes algebraic $\Rightarrow$ Kuga–Satake algebraic").

So the honest content of (c) is as follows. For real quadratic $E$, there is an exact reduction to a similitude statement (Theorem C). For non-multiquadratic $E$, there is a proof that $H^2$-level similitude statements do not suffice (Theorem A), and nothing more.

## 6. GAPS (explicit)

**G1 — the top target.**
- *Needed:* $E^{\mathrm{alg}}=E$ for every projective K3 surface $S$ with RM by $E$ (Lemma 1.3).
- *Why I cannot prove it:* no construction known to me produces an algebraic element of $E\setminus E^{(2)}$, for any $S$ (§4.4).
- Theorem A shows that $H^2$-level similitude chains cannot. Remark 3.7 shows that such an element needs a surface in $S\times S$ of geometric genus $\ge2$ carrying two different copies of $T$.
- Remark 3.6(c) shows that an algebraic class $Q_f$ in $H^4$ of a hyper-Kähler fourfold would suffice. Its algebraicity is again an open instance of the Hodge conjecture, equivalent to the target when $X=S^{[2]}$.
- The Kuga–Satake correspondence is known to be algebraic only on special families with $\rho=16$. There $m\cdot[E:\mathbb Q]=6$ and $m\ge3$, so $E$ is at most quadratic.

**G2 — the real quadratic case in general.**
- *Needed:* $D\in\Lambda(S)$, i.e. one algebraic Hodge similitude of multiplier $Dc^{2}$ out of $T$ (Theorem C). Equivalently, by Proposition 4.1: every rational Hodge similitude of multiplier $D$ from $T$ to the transcendental lattice of a projective K3 surface is algebraic.
- *Known cases:* §4.4.
- *Why I cannot prove the rest:*
  - Exact equivalences of (twisted) derived categories induce isometries (Mukai, Orlov, Huybrechts–Stellari), so Buskin's and Huybrechts' method yields only isometries.
  - Fourier–Mukai functors that are *not* equivalences can realise non-square multipliers. A kernel supported on the graph of a finite map does so, with multiplier the degree (Lemma 3.3). *Correction:* round 1 said that "no categorical construction can realise a non-square multiplier", which was false.
  - Algebraic Kuga–Satake correspondences also realise them (§4.4).
  - Finite symplectic group actions give only $D\in\{2,3,6\}$ and need $\rho\ge9$, $13$, $15$ respectively. For $D=2,3$ they reduce to Varesco's theorem (Corollary 4.2(b)).
  - The examples where none of this applies include $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ with $\rho\le14$ (§4.4). *Correction:* round 1 listed Elsenhans–Jahnel's $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ family as open; it is covered by Schlickewei.
  - A dominant rational map of degree $Dc^2$ between a K3 surface isogenous to $S$ and another K3 surface would close a case (Theorem C′). I know of none outside §4.4.

**G3 — existence of examples for Corollary 4.2(b) with $D=6$.** I have not verified that K3 surfaces with RM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$ and a symplectic $S_3$-action exist. This would need:
- an even lattice $NS$ of rank $16$ containing the rank-$14$ coinvariant lattice of $S_3$;
- whose orthogonal complement $T$ of rank $6$ is rationally the transfer of a ternary form over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)$ with signatures $(2,1)$ and $(0,3)$ at the two real places.

Corollary 4.2(b) is a correct implication, but it may be vacuous for $D=6$. That is the only case in which it could be new.

**G5 — Kuga–Satake (Remark 5.3(b))** is quoted, not proved. No theorem above depends on it.

**G6 — the round-1 headline obstruction (withdrawn; moved here in round 2).**
- *What was claimed.* Corollary 3.4's gloss and the round-1 verdict: "no combination of Buskin's theorem, Huybrechts' isogenies, twisted Fourier–Mukai partners, Mukai's moduli-space correspondences, Markman's theorem, finite covers and quotients, and their transposes can prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$", and "this strategy can never prove the Hodge conjecture for $S\times S$ unless $E$ is multiquadratic". Also Remark 3.6's round-1 conclusion, "passing through $H^4$ of a moduli space does not help".
- *The referees' objection, stated fairly.* Lemma 3.2 lists only $H^2$-level maps. Correspondences built from the same tools act on $T$ through $H^{2k}(X)$ with $k\ge2$. Examples are other Künneth components of Chern classes of (twisted) universal families on $S\times M$, Markman's cycles composed with cycles of higher codimension, and cup products. Round-1 Remark 3.6 treated only maps $T\to H^4$ for $K3^{[2]}$-type. It *assumed* the factors $\phi_i$ were similitudes, which is exactly the unproved point. For $K3^{[n]}$, $n\ge3$, one has $\operatorname{Sym}^3T\supset T$ via $x\mapsto x\cdot Q$.
- *Assessment.* The objection is correct, and Remark 3.6(c) now proves it: a route through $H^6$ of a $K3^{[2]}$-type fourfold realises every $e\in E$, given one algebraic Hodge class $Q_f\in H^4$.
- *What would restore a weaker claim.* One would need to determine the subring of algebraic classes on hyper-Kähler manifolds generated by the tools (Chern classes of (twisted) universal families, Markman's cycles, graphs), and show that its action on $T$ stays in $E^{(2)}$. I do not know how to do this, and it may be false. For $X=S^{[2]}$ it contains the original problem.
